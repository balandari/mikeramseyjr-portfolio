# Chapter Two — The Front Room

*Calder, Kentucky · October 1946*

Nell was nine that October. The dish was a chicken pie. Her mother made the best chicken pie on either street, and everybody said so, and it was a chicken pie, not a chicken pot pie. Her mother had opinions about chicken pot pie. A chicken pot pie was a stew with a lid of dough over it, full of carrots and peas and potatoes to stretch the chicken, and you ate it with a spoon. A chicken pie had a crust under and a crust over and nothing between them but a whole hen's worth of chicken and its gravy, baked in the deep blue dish until it would cut in a slice and hold, and it took the better part of a morning.

The crust was the part Nell liked the most, rolled thin and crimped shut all the way around with the tines of a fork, the marks as even as stitches, with four slits cut in the top for the steam, and it had come out of the oven browned and blistered with the gravy bubbled up gold through the slits.

Nell had been allowed to do the fork on it once, when she was seven. Her marks had wandered, close together on one side and far apart on the other, and she had seen it herself before her mother did, and had known just where she had gone wrong, pressing too hard when she hurried and too light when she was careful. Her mother had looked at them and taken the fork back and gone around the whole crust again without a word, because a pie that was going out the door with the Hale name on it did not go out with a crooked crust, and Nell had not been asked to do it since.

She had had two years to think about it. She knew how to hold the fork and how to keep her count. She would not miss. If she were only given another chance.

The kitchen was hot from the oven and full of the smell of the pie, and Nell's mouth watered at it and her heart sank a little at the same time, because a pie like that on a weekday was not for them. Under the pie smell was the lye soap, the Saturday smell, the floor-scrubbing smell that stung her nose and drew the skin of her hands tight, and it did not belong on a Wednesday. The good smell and the hard smell together made her uneasy, because she knew what they meant when they came on the same day. Her mother baked and scrubbed when somebody on the street was dying. It was what she did instead of talking about it.

The window over the sink was propped open to let the heat out, and through it Nell could see the wash on the line, gone stiff in the sun, and the sycamore letting its leaves down yellow one at a time into the yard.

The pie was for the Laceys on Front Street. Mr. Lacey was dying, and when somebody on Water Street or Front Street was dying, the women cooked and the children carried. That was just the way it was. Her mother had done it for the Suttons when old Mrs. Sutton went, and for the Meeks when the baby died, and now it was the Laceys, and Nell was old enough.

Her mother wrapped the dish in a clean towel and set it in her hands and looked at her over it. Her mother was a tall woman, taller than most of the men on Water Street, with her hair pinned up hard and a face that did not give away much, and she had a look she gave you over the top of whatever she was handing you that meant she was about to tell you how it was to be done.

"You carry it flat, with both hands, and you don't run. You give it to Mrs. Lacey and you say your mother sends it and you come straight back. You don't go in past the kitchen. You hear me?"

"Yes ma'am."

"Straight back."

"Yes ma'am."

Roy was under the table playing with a wooden truck, making the engine noise in his throat, the one all boys made and no girl could. Frank was asleep on the daybed in the front room. He was three and still sucked his thumb, and nobody had the heart to stop him. Her father was gone on the freight to Corbin and would not be home until Thursday. She went out the back door with the pie and down the two steps and along the side of the house to the street.

It was October and the river was low. From the top of the bank on Water Street you could see it, a wide brown road going past with the Indiana shore on the far side and the sandbars showing, and a tow going down with six barges of coal, so slow that the smoke from its stack stood up in the air behind it. The houses on Water Street sat on the high side with their porches facing the water, and the ground fell away across the street to the landing, where the ferry had tied up before the bridge was built and where boys still fished off the pilings. A quarter mile downstream, where the river bent, a spit of sand ran out from the bank, Tolliver's Bar, where every child on Water Street swam from May to September and nobody was swimming now, with the water down and the sand gone gray in the cold. Behind Water Street, up a short steep block, was Front Street, where the houses were a little bigger and had a little more paint on them and the river had come into them only once, in the year she was born, instead of every few years.

The Lacey house was the gray one with the porch swing. The swing was where Mr. Lacey sat in the evenings after supper, and where he raised his hand to her when she went by, and it was chained up now, pulled tight to the wall with a length of chain, because the wind came up off the river in the afternoons and an empty swing banged against a house, and that was not a noise a family wanted with a dying man in the front room. That was the reason people gave. Nell thought there was another reason, one that nobody said. An empty swing moving in the wind looked like somebody sitting in it, and the somebody who belonged in that one was in the front room dying, and nobody in that house could have stood to look out the window and see him there. Nell knew what the chain meant. Everybody on Water Street knew. She went up the steps with the pie held out in front of her and knocked with her elbow, and waited, and knocked again. Mrs. Lacey's sister opened the door. Nell had never seen her before. She was a heavy woman in an apron with her sleeves pushed up, and she was not from Calder; she had come from somewhere down the river, people said, to help her sister see it through, and she knew Lila's name from the kitchen and did not know Lila's girl at all. She looked down at Nell and at the pie, and stepped back out of the doorway and said, "Come in, come in," and Nell came over the sill into the hall with the dish held out in front of her.

"Is that Lila's?" She looked at the dish for a second before she looked at Nell, and her eyes were red, and she seemed to have to come back from somewhere to do it.

"Yes ma'am. My mother sends it."

"That's kind of her. You tell her I said thank you." She took the dish out of the towel and folded the towel over once and put it back in Nell's hands. "You tell her we appreciate it."

"Yes ma'am."

She touched Nell's shoulder once, and turned and went back down the hall toward the kitchen with the pie, and Nell was left standing in the front hall holding the towel, with the door open behind her and the October light coming in around her on the floor.

The hall smelled of camphor and of something else under it, sweetish and heavy. There was a hat rack with a man's hat on it and a coat that nobody would wear again. The kitchen was at the back and it was full of women. She could hear them, low, the voices starting and stopping and starting again, the same few words coming round: he took a little broth, the doctor said, we'll see by morning. She could pick out Mrs. Lacey's voice, high and worn down, and the sister's, and a third she could not place, maybe Mrs. Meek. Somebody set a cup down in a saucer. Nobody was coming back for her. She was supposed to turn around and go down the steps and along the block and back home.

The front room was to her right, through a doorway with a curtain hung across it on a rod and drawn halfway, and it was quiet in there. Not silent. Quiet. There was a sound in it she could not make out, slow, and she stood in the hall and listened to it.

She did not decide to go in. The quiet in that room pulled her, and she put the curtain aside with the back of the hand that held the towel and went through, and that was all she could have said about it, then or that night in bed with Roy breathing across the room and the sycamore scraping the window. It pulled her. Her mother had told her not to. She had heard her mother. She went in anyway.

The front room had the good furniture in it, pushed back against the walls to make space, and in the space they had set up an iron bed under the front window where the light was. Mr. Lacey was in it. She had known him only as the man in the porch swing, the one chained to the wall now, who raised his hand when she went by, a big man with a stomach and a watch chain who sat square in the middle of the swing and made the chains creak, and who always had a smile for her. She knew it was him. There was nobody else it could be. But the man in the bed had nothing left of the man in the swing, and it took her a moment, standing in the doorway, to put the two of them together. He was lying on his back with his mouth open and his hands on top of the blanket, and the stomach was gone, and the rest of him had gone with it. All his weight was gone. He had wasted away to nothing, and he looked like a skeleton with skin laid over it. She was ashamed of thinking it. This was the man who had waved to her from that porch every time she went by of an evening and he was in the swing, which was most evenings, and smiled when he did it, a slow wide smile like he had been waiting all day for her to come by, and once, when she was small, had called her over to the rail and given her a peppermint out of his vest pocket and told her not to tell her mother. She had liked him. Everybody had. But this was what she saw, and she could not make herself see anything else. His cheeks had fallen in and his eyes had sunk back and the bones of his hands stood up under the skin like sticks under a cloth, and he was breathing in a slow, dragging pull that took a long time going in and a long time coming out, with a wait in between that seemed to get longer every time. That was the sound she had heard from the hall. She had never heard anyone breathe like that. Nobody was in the room with him. He was by himself with that sound, with his wife and her sister and the neighbor women twenty feet away in the kitchen, and it was the saddest thing she had ever seen. She did not know what she meant to do about it. She only knew she was not going to leave him there alone.

So she stood by the bed. Her shoes had brought a leaf in on them and she could see it on the rug, and she thought that her mother would have something to say about that. His eyes were open a little and were not looking at anything. There was a glass of water on the table by the bed with a spoon in it, and a bottle with a label, and a Bible with a ribbon in it, and a dish with a wet cloth folded in it. His hand on the blanket was big and spotted, with the knuckles standing up and the nails thick and yellow, and she put her hand on top of it, because it seemed wrong for it to be lying there by itself. It was cold. She had expected warm.

There was a clock on the mantel across the room, a black one with a gold face and a pendulum you could see through a little glass door, and it ticked loud in the quiet. She found herself watching its second hand and counting his breaths against it, not for any reason she could have given, just because the wait between them was so long that she wanted to know how long. She got to eleven in a minute. She did it again. Nine. Then twelve, and then eight, and once a wait so long she thought it was the last one and it was not. She did not know how many a person was supposed to have, but she knew it was more than that, and she knew they were not supposed to come and go like that, and it frightened her in a way she did not have words for. She did not know what to think about it. She could not stand there saying nothing, either, with him breathing like that, so she said the only thing that came to her, the thing her mother said to Frank when he woke in the night.

"It's all right," she said to him, very low, so the kitchen wouldn't hear. She did not know if it was. "It's all right. I'm here."

She stayed. She could hear the women in the kitchen and did not go to them. They were talking about him, and none of them knew what was happening in this room, that a girl was in here with him holding his hand and counting, and that his breaths were coming and going while they talked. Somebody laughed once, short, and stopped, as if she had caught herself.

A truck went by on Front Street, and the window glass hummed with it, and the driver went on to wherever he was going knowing nothing of this room, and the sound of him faded up the hill.

The light came in the window across the blanket and across his hands and hers, and she saw that her hand was small on his and that her nails were dirty from the yard, and she did not take it away.

His breathing went to eight. Then she lost the count, because she was looking at his face, and it seemed to her that something in it had eased, around the mouth, some small thing she had no word for, and then she heard the front door, and her mother's voice in the hall, asking the sister where Nell was, and then her mother's voice saying her name, sharp, the voice for when she was in trouble. She had been gone longer than it took to walk to Front Street and back. Long enough for her mother to put down what she was doing and come up the block after her.

Her mother came through the curtain and stopped.

Nell did not take her hand away.

"Eleanor Hale." It came out low, almost a whisper, and harder for being low. Her mother's face was red under the pins, and her eyes went once to the curtain and the kitchen beyond it, and once to Nell's hand on top of Mr. Lacey's, and came back. "What did I tell you."

"He was by himself."

"That is not your business." Still low, and slower now, each word coming out on its own. "That is not any business of yours. You get out here this minute."

Nell's face went hot. She was not sorry, and she knew she was supposed to be, and the two things together made it hard to look at her mother, so she looked at Mr. Lacey instead, at the hand under hers, and wished her mother would look at it too and see what she had seen. Her mother did not look at it. She looked at Nell.

She got up. She set Mr. Lacey's hand back on the blanket, palm down, the fingers a little apart, where it had been when she found it, and she looked at his face once more and then she went. Her mother had her by the arm above the elbow before she was through the curtain. She marched her past the kitchen door, where the women had gone quiet and were looking, out onto the porch, down the steps, and along the block, and did not let go of her arm until they were around the corner onto Water Street and out of sight of the house.

Nell followed, as she had no other choice. She did not try to get her arm back, because it was her mother's hand, and she had never in her life pulled away from her mother's hand and could not have made herself start now. But she did not understand what she had done, and all the way along the block she waited for her mother to tell her, and the not understanding was worse than the grip on her arm.

"You don't go in a room like that. You don't ever go in a room like that. A child. In a dying man's room with his wife in the kitchen. What will people say."

"He was by himself, Mama."

"He has a wife. He has a sister-in-law. He has a house full of people."

"They were in the kitchen."

Her mother stopped walking and looked at her, and Nell braced for whatever was coming. Nothing came. Her mother looked away, down the street toward their own house, where Roy had come out on the porch to see what the noise was, and her mouth worked, and she let go of Nell's arm.

"Go on home," she said. "Go on."

Then she turned around and went back along the block toward Front Street with her shoulders set, and Nell understood that she was going back to the Laceys' kitchen to stand in front of those women and say whatever had to be said to make it right, and that this was the part her mother had been dreading the whole way down the block.

Nell went. At supper her mother did not speak to her, but she put the plate down in front of her the same as always, and she had saved her the piece of cornbread from the end of the pan that Nell liked, and Roy looked from one of them to the other and for once said nothing.

Nell took herself up to bed early without being told. She lay in the dark and listened to her mother moving in the kitchen, and thought about the front room. She thought about the sound.

After a while the stairs creaked, and her mother came to the door of the room and stood there. Nell kept her eyes shut and her breathing slow, and waited for her mother to say something. She didn't. The floor creaked closer. The blanket came up over her shoulder, and a hand rested on it, and stayed a second longer than it needed to, and lifted. The floor creaked away. The stairs creaked. Nell lay a long time with her eyes shut and did not move, so as not to lose the place where the hand had been.

Mr. Lacey died that night. Nell heard it at school the next day, on the steps, from a Lacey cousin who had heard it from the women in the kitchen: he had gone about suppertime, with everybody there, Mrs. Lacey holding his hand. Nell listened and told the cousin she was sorry, and went in to her desk.

She did not believe that Mrs. Lacey had been there to hold his hand. She wanted to. But she had been in that room, and she knew where everybody else had been, and she did not think so. She did not say it.

She thought about Mr. Lacey's hand. She thought that when she had put it back on the blanket it had been a little warmer than when she had picked it up, and she did not know what to make of that, and she did not tell anybody, then or ever.
