# Chapter Three — The Twenty-Fourth of January

*Calder, Kentucky · January 1947*

Nell woke on the morning of her tenth birthday to the smell of the cake already in the oven, and lay there a minute with her eyes shut and let it be ten.

She had been waiting for ten since the day she turned nine. Ten was two numbers. Ten was out of the little-girl years and into whatever came after them. She had lain awake the night before, listening to Roy breathe across the room and the sycamore scrape the window, and waited for something to happen at midnight, some change she would feel go through her. Nothing had. She had gone to sleep still nine. Now it was morning and the cake was in and she was ten, and she felt exactly as she had the day before, and was a little let down by that. The next number that mattered was thirteen, when she would be a teenager, which was a thing she could picture, girls at the high school with their hair up and their own money, and could not wait for, and it was a long way off, and not as far as it had been yesterday.

The cake smell meant her mother had been up before the light. The yellow layers were in. The caramel icing would come in the afternoon, and the icing would go wrong. It always went wrong.

It was the only cake her mother ever made for Nell. Roy got a chocolate cake on his birthday and Frank got whatever he asked for, and both of those came out fine, because Nell's mother could bake anything she set her hand to. Her biscuits came up light, her pie crust was talked about on two streets, and her cornbread had a crust on it you could hear. But once a year she got out the good mixing bowl and made a yellow layer cake, and the layers came out fine, high and even and gold, because she did everything right.

Then she stood at the stove and made the caramel icing. Caramel icing was sugar and butter and milk boiled together and stirred and stirred until it thickened, and it went to sugar in the pan if you took your eye off it for a minute.

She tried. Nell had watched her try. One year she bought the good sugar from Louisville. One year she made Nell stand beside her and count the minutes. It made no difference.

Nell knew why it was the icing and not the layers. She had had ten years to watch. The layers were work, and her mother could do work on any day of the year without looking up. Caramel icing was the one thing in her mother's kitchen that could not be done moving. You stood in one place with a spoon and stirred for ten minutes and did nothing else, and your hands were busy and your eyes were not, and the window over the sink was in front of you, looking out across the yard at the river, and on the twenty-fourth of January that was more standing still than her mother could manage. She would be stirring, and then she would be standing with the spoon gone still and her face turned to the window, looking at nothing Nell could see, and the icing would go grainy in the pan behind her, and she would come back to it and say, "Well," and spread it on anyway, and it would set on the cake in a rough gray crust that broke when you cut it.

It was not that she could not make caramel icing. Nell had seen her make it for other people's cakes and get it right. It was that she could not make it on that day, and Nell knew where her mother went when she looked at the river on that day. She went to the night in the story.

Nell knew what the cake was for, too. It came on the day she was born, and the story came right after it, every year, and nobody had to say that the two went together. The cake was for the day she was kept, which was her mother's word for it and nobody else's. What she had never worked out was why it was always that cake and never a better one, since her mother did not do things badly, and did not do things at all if she could not do them right. She had a guess. She thought it might have been the cake her mother had meant to make that January, for something that was supposed to happen the week Nell was born and never got to because of the river, a christening, or a first Sunday, or only a table with the neighbors at it, and that making it every year since, and getting it wrong, was her mother's way of keeping the date. She did not know. It was the kind of thing her mother would not have said if you asked her outright, and Nell was her mother's daughter and never asked.

Every year they ate it, and every year nobody at the table said a word about it. Her father ate two pieces, slowly, and said it was good, and her mother said, "It is not," and that was the whole of the conversation about the cake, the same words every year, like a thing the two of them had agreed on long ago.

Nell did not mind that it went wrong. She had never minded. The icing came out gray and gritty and broke when you cut it, and it was hers, the one cake in that house that was, and she had understood young that the wrongness of it was part of the day, the same as the story that came after the cake was part of the day. What Nell could not stand was her mother's face at the moment the icing turned in the pan. Her mother never said a word about it. She did not have to. Nell had been watching that face at that moment for as long as she could remember, and it was the one time in the year her mother looked beaten.

It was three months since Mr. Lacey. Three months since she had stood in the front room with his cold hand under hers and counted his breaths against the clock, and heard that they came and went, and told him it was all right, and been marched out by the arm. Three months since the night her mother had come up the stairs and pulled the blanket over her shoulder and said nothing. Nell still thought about the front room. She thought about the sound. She had not told anyone that the hand had been warmer when she set it down, and she did not think she ever would. Something in the house had been different since. Nell could not have said what. Her mother looked at her, sometimes, when she thought Nell was not looking, and Nell looked at her mother, and neither of them said a word about it, and it was there at the table every night between the two of them, and nobody else could see it.

Her father was home. She had heard his boots on the porch in the night, late, and the door, and her mother's voice low in the kitchen, and had gone back to sleep knowing he had made it. He always made it. He was gone two and three days at a time on the freights and you could not tell him when he would be back, but however the runs fell he was at the table on the twenty-fourth of January, and nobody remarked on that. She came down in her stockings and he was at the table with his coffee and his railroad coat still on the back of the chair, and he looked up and said, "Sister," and nothing else, and that was her birthday from him and it was enough. Roy was under the table already. Frank was in his chair with a spoon. Her mother was at the stove with her back to the room and the two yellow layers on the rack beside her, cooling, and she did not turn around, and Nell had not expected her to.

Nell had made up her mind about one thing, without exactly making it up. When the icing went on the stove that afternoon, she was going to be standing at her mother's elbow. She did not know what she thought she could do there. She had stood there once before, at eight, when her mother made her count, and it had done no good. She was going to stand there anyway.

That afternoon, it came out right.

When her mother put the pan on the stove, Nell went and stood at her elbow, as she had told herself that morning she would. Nobody had asked her to. She had not felt ten all day, and she felt it then, walking across the kitchen to stand where she had decided to stand, in the place where she had stood at eight when her mother made her count the minutes and it had done no good. Her mother looked at her, and did not send her away. Her mother stirred. Nell watched the second hand on the kitchen clock. And it happened as it always happened. The spoon slowed and went still, and her mother's face turned toward the window over the sink and the river beyond it, and Nell watched the sugar start to grain at the edge of the pan, and said, "Mama."

Only that. Her mother's face came back from the window. She looked down at the pan and stirred, fast, because there was no time for anything else, and her free hand came down on Nell's shoulder and stayed there, gripping a little, while she stirred. It had not gone too far to bring back. The graining smoothed out under the spoon and the icing came up thick and gold, and at the second it did, Nell felt her mother's hand tighten on her shoulder, once, hard, and then ease. Her mother did not look up from the pan. Nell did not look up from it either. They stood and watched it come right, and Nell knew, and knew her mother knew, that it had never come right before, not once in ten years, and that it had this time because of a word.

Her mother spread the icing over the layers with the flat of the knife, and it went on smooth and set as it was supposed to set, with a shine on it. She stood and looked at it a moment with the empty pan in her hand. Then she looked at Nell, and smiled at her. It was a small smile and it did not last. Her mother had smiled at her before, at things she said, at her in a new dress, but not like this, not for something Nell had done, not between the two of them as if they had done it together. She set the pan on the stove and put her hand against Nell's cheek, soft, the palm still warm from the pan, and held it there with her eyes on Nell's, as if there were something she knew about her. Then she let her hand fall, and, because she was who she was, turned to the sink and ran the water. Nell stood where she had been left and did not move.

Her mother had touched her face before, to wash it, or to lay the back of her hand against it for fever, but that was hands doing a job. This was not a job. It was the first time Nell could remember her mother's hand on her face for no reason but Nell, and it had lasted a second, and something in her chest had come loose at it and was still loose. She wanted to cry and did not, because her mother was three feet away and would have heard.

In the front room she had gone to a man who was alone, and her mother had pulled her out by the arm and told her it was not her business. At the stove her mother had gone to that window every year of Nell's life with nobody watching her go, because nobody had known she went anywhere. The year Nell was eight she had stood right there and watched the clock, because she was told to, and had not seen her mother leave. This year she had stood there because she chose to, and watched the clock and her mother both, and seen her mother go, and said one word, and her mother had come back. The hand on her face said that it was all right. Nell thought it said more than that. She thought it said that it had been all right in the front room too, with her hand on Mr. Lacey's, and that her mother had known it then and could not say so, and had said it with her hand now instead. She could not have proved that. She believed it.

She stood with the water running and her mother's back to her and felt the warm place on her cheek go slowly cool, and did not want it to, and let it.

At supper Roy, who noticed everything and said most of it, took one bite and said, "It's not gritty," and looked at his mother as if she had done a trick. Her mother said, "No." Frank ate his with both hands. Her father ate two pieces, slowly, and said it was good, and there was a wait, longer than the wait every other year, and her mother said, "It is," and looked across the table at Nell when she said it.

Nell looked back at her. She would not have, a year ago; she would have looked at her plate. But there was a thing between the two of them now that had not been there at breakfast, and she held her mother's eyes with it across the table, with her father and the boys right there and none of them knowing, and her mother held hers, and then went back to her cake. Her mother had said "It is." In ten years Nell had never heard her say it.

It was a tradition in that house that every child got the story of the day they were born, once a year, at the table, after the cake. Roy's was a hot night in August with the doctor late and her father pacing the porch. Frank's was the night her father made it home from Corbin with ten minutes to spare and came in the door still in his railroad coat, and Frank had been born into that coat, or near enough, and her mother told it every year to make her father laugh, and he did. Nell's was the flood. Her mother told it because it was Nell's, the same as she told the boys theirs, and it was the only one of the three with a stranger in it.

Her father sat at the head of the table with his face all lines from the railroad and the weather, a man who could look at the river from the porch for an hour and not say a word, and who had called her Sister since before the boys came and had never stopped. Roy sat on his right, seven that winter, a quick dark boy who could not sit still and climbed things and was the only one of them who could make their father laugh. Frank sat on a cushion on his left, three, and knew he was the baby, and could get out of anything with a look, and they all let him, and pretended they didn't.

That night her mother set her fork down and placed her hands flat on the table on either side of her plate, and the boys knew from the hands what was coming and looked at the cake, and her father looked at his plate. That much was every year. Every year the hands stayed pressed to the table from the first word to the last, and the voice was held level on purpose the whole way through, because there was too much under it to let any out at a supper table with three children looking at her, and Nell, who had watched those hands since she was small enough to have to look up at the table, had never once seen them move.

That night her mother said the first sentence, which was the same every year, "It rained the whole of January that year," and then, instead of telling it to the middle of the table, she told it to Nell. She looked at her the whole way through. Nell had heard the story for as long as she could remember, and this was the first time her mother had ever told it like this, to her, and she sat very still and looked back, with the thing from the stove still between them, and let her mother tell it to her.

It went like this.

It had rained the whole of January over the entire valley, and the river had come up over the landing and over Water Street and into the houses, and it had not stopped there. It had come up the stairs, a step at a time, and nobody could have told you where it meant to stop.

Nell had been three days old. Her mother said that looking at her, and Nell understood, in a way she had not the other nine times, that the baby in the story was her, sitting there with cake on her plate.

She had been born in the front bedroom, with the rain on the roof and the river already over the landing. The doctor could not get through from town. Mrs. Sutton from two doors down had come in her apron with her kettle and delivered the baby herself, and when it was done she had gone back home through the rain to her own house and her own children, because she had a house and children too. Nell's father had been out on the railroad, and the water had cut the line below town, and nobody knew where he was or when he would get back. So her mother had been alone in the house, three days out of childbed and not yet steady on her feet, with a baby three days old, when the water came in under the kitchen door. She had gathered up the baby, a lamp, a blanket, what food she could carry, and gone up the stairs. The water had come up the stairs after her, a step at a time, through the night and the next day. What it had been like, her mother never said. She said what happened.

Her mother had stood at the upstairs window, the one in the front bedroom that looked out on the water, with Nell in a blanket, and the water a foot below the sill. And a man in a johnboat had come along the row of houses in the rain taking people out of the windows. An old man, her mother said. She never knew his name. He came to the window and held his arms out.

Her mother always said that she stood there with the baby against her and could not make her arms do it. A stranger, in a boat, in the rain, with the river going by under him. That night she said it to Nell's face, and her eyes did not leave Nell's while she said it, and Nell felt what her mother had held onto at that window, because it was her. And then her mother, at that window, had looked at the old man's face. It was an old man's face that had done this all day and would do it all night, and there was nothing in it to be afraid of. There was the water a foot below the sill. She handed Nell down to him, not a week old.

He took her in the crook of his arm and looked at her and said, "She'll live." Her mother said those two words across the table into Nell's eyes, and something went through Nell that she could not have named, as if the old man had said them to her and not about her. Then her mother went on. He put her in the bottom of the boat on a folded coat, and then helped her mother climb out over the sill and into the boat after her, and went on to the next window.

"You were kept," her mother said at the end of it, as she said every year, and every other year she picked up her fork.

That night, when she came to the window and the old man and handing the baby down, her right hand had come up off the table an inch and gone back down flat, and Nell had seen it, and did not think anyone else had. And at the end her mother did not pick up her fork. She sat with her hands flat on the table and looked across it at Nell, and the boys went still, because they knew the story was over and their mother was not done, and her father looked up from his plate for the first time in ten tellings. Her mother said, in the same flat voice, "I had made up my mind you were going to die. On the stairs, the second night. I had already decided it, so it would not be so bad when it came. And that old man looked at you for about a second and said you'd live, and I believed him over myself. I never saw him again to tell him he was right." Then she looked at her plate, and picked up her fork, and the story was over, and she never added that part again.

Nobody moved. Nell sat with her hands in her lap and could not have got a word out if she had been asked for one. Her mother had decided she would die. Her mother had sat on the stairs in the dark with the water coming up and made up her mind to it, so it would not hurt so much, and Nell had never in her life heard her mother say she had believed anything, and she had believed a stranger. Her mother had set the fork down again without eating. Nell looked at her hands on the table. They were flat again, pressed, the knuckles white. She wanted to put her own hand on them and did not know if she was allowed.

Her father did. He reached across the corner of the table and put his hand over her mother's, the whole of it, and left it there, and her mother did not move hers out from under it, and did not look up, and nobody at that table had ever seen either of those things. Roy looked at Frank. Frank, who was three, looked at the cake. After a while her mother said, "Eat your cake," to the boys, and her father took his hand back, and they ate their cake.

They lived in the house on Water Street that the river had come into, a narrow two-story frame house with a porch across the front and a kitchen across the back, and in the upstairs hall there was a line on the wallpaper, faint brown, at the height of a tall man's head, where the water had stopped. Her mother had painted over it twice. It came back through both times. After the second time her mother left it.

Nell went up those stairs that night past the line on the wallpaper and lay in bed and did not sleep for a long time. She thought about the stairs with the water on them. She thought about her mother sitting on them in the dark with a lamp and a baby, deciding. She thought about the word. Kept. Not saved. Everybody at church said saved, and her mother never did, not about this. Kept, as if somebody had held onto her, and never a word about who, or what for. Other babies that winter had not been; her mother did not say so, but Calder was a small town and you heard things. She did not think her mother meant that God had reached down. Her mother did not talk that way and did not like people who did. She thought her mother meant only that it had happened, and that it was Nell's now, and Nell lay in the dark with it and did not know what to do with it, and fell asleep with the warm place on her cheek long gone and still there.
