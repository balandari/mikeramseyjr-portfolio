# Chapter Four — The River

*Calder, Kentucky · July 1951*

The kitchen was no place for anybody in July, and on the afternoon of the beans, especially not little boys.

Her mother had the canner going on the stove. The jars were lined up on the table with their lids beside them. The windows were open and no air came in, and the whole house smelled of green beans, vinegar, and hot glass. Nell had been snapping beans since before breakfast. Roy had been sent out of the kitchen three times and had come back three times, because Roy was eleven and could not be anywhere he was told to be. The third time, her mother took the colander out of Nell's hands and said, "Take them to the bar. Both of them. And watch them."

Nell had been going down to the bar, which was the sandbar a quarter mile below town where the children swam, since she was five, first with the Sutton girls and then on her own. The Suttons were two doors down and had a child for every age of Hale and then some, girls older than Nell and boys the age of Frank, and Mrs. Sutton had delivered Nell in the flood, so the two houses ran in and out of each other and always had. Nell had carried Roy down there on her hip before he could swim and taught him to swim there when he was six. The boys went most days. Her mother had gone there herself as a girl, in the same water.

So she took them. She got her library book off the shelf by the door, because if she was going to sit on the sand all afternoon she was going to read, and she put her hair up, which she had started doing that summer, and they went.

The book was a horse story, the second one that week. The library in Calder was one room over the post office, open Tuesdays and Saturdays, and Miss Ada Coyle, who ran the post office downstairs, ran the library upstairs too, and let Nell take two books at a time when the rule was one, because Nell brought them back.

Nell was fourteen that summer. She had been a teenager for a year and a half, and she had waited for it the whole of her life.

Ten had been two numbers, and she had wanted ten. But ten had turned out to be nine with a different name, and she had known even at ten that the real line was thirteen, because thirteen was where you stopped being a child. She had known teenagers all her life. The Sutton girls had been teenagers when Nell was small, tall girls with their hair up who walked to the high school in a group and laughed at things Nell could not hear and did not have to mind their mothers about everything anymore, and she had watched the oldest one go all the way through it and come out the other side married and gone to Louisville, a grown woman, which was how you knew the teenage years were a real place, because people went in one end and came out the other end different. Nobody Nell knew had ever come out of being ten different.

And there was a word for it now. There had not always been. Miss Ada Coyle kept the magazines on the table under the library window, and Nell had read in one of them, when she was eleven, that there were six million teen-age girls in America living in a wonderful world of their own, with a picture of them in sweaters, and had understood that the world she was going to live in had already been named and was waiting for her to be old enough to get in.

So she had lain awake on the last night of twelve, in the January of 1950, with Roy breathing across the room, and waited at midnight for it, sure this time. Ten had been practice. This was the crossing. She had said the word to herself in the dark, teenager, and waited to feel it happen.

She had felt the same nothing.

In the morning she had been thirteen and had gone down and eaten her breakfast and gone to school, and nobody at school had treated her any differently, and the high school girls had walked past in their group with their hair up and had not looked at her, and she had understood that being thirteen did not get you in. Something else did. She had thought for a while that spring that it might be the other thing, the thing the Sutton girls had, which had come to her in April and which her mother had seen to in the pantry with a box from the drugstore and four sentences, and which Nell did not think about if she could help it. It had not got her in either. It had only made her tired one week in four and careful about the rest. She had put her hair up anyway, the first warm day this summer, a year later, with two pins from her mother's dresser. Her mother had looked at it and said nothing. Roy had said she looked like a schoolteacher. It had not made her any different that she could tell. She was still Sister. She was still the one sent down to the bar with the little boys and a book.

She had the book under her arm with sand already in the spine from the bar.

They walked down in the heat, along Water Street and past the last house and down the path through the willows. Frank, who was eight, talked the whole way about a snapping turtle somebody had seen at the landing, big as a washtub, that would take your foot off. Roy walked ahead of them with a towel around his neck and did not listen to any of it. He had grown that year. He was all knees and elbows and brown as a nut, and he was in a hurry to be somewhere, which was how he was about everything, and once, on the path, he turned around and walked backward and told Frank the turtle had been a rock, and Frank said it had not, and Roy laughed at him and turned back around and went on ahead.

He waited for her where the path came out of the willows, which he had not done since he was small, and when she caught up he reached over and pulled the pin out of her hair so it came down, and ran. She chased him ten yards across the hot sand in her sandals and gave up and put her hair back up with the pin he had dropped, and he stood at the water's edge grinning at her with the towel around his neck, eleven years old and pleased with himself, and she could not stay mad at him and never had been able to.

"Pool side," she said. "Both of you." It came out in their mother's voice, the short one that did not expect an answer, and she heard it as it left her, and so did Roy. "Yes, Mama," he said, and Frank laughed out loud, because Roy never let her get away with sounding like their mother and she did it more than she liked. Then he went down to the pool with the Sutton boys, and she went up to the drift logs with her book. It was one of those July afternoons when the haze took all the blue out of the sky and left it white from one bank to the other, and the cicadas did not stop.

Tolliver's Bar was a spit of sand and shale that ran out from the Kentucky bank a quarter mile below town, where the river bent. You came out of the willows onto the sand, and the sand was hot enough to hurt through your sandals until you got to the top of the bar where the drift logs were. On the upstream side the water lay in a slack pool that was warm by July and shallow enough for the little ones to stand in, with a mud bottom that squeezed up between your toes. On the downstream side, past the point, the current came around the bend hard and deep and brown and went by fast enough that you could see it move, with sticks in it, and foam, and sometimes a whole tree turning slowly with its roots in the air. Everybody knew which side you swam on. Nell had known it before she could swim. So had Roy.

She sat on the sand at the top of the bar with her back against a bleached log the river had left there, and read. Frank was in the pool with two Sutton boys, doing what eight-year-olds did in water, which was mostly shouting and splashing. Roy was eleven, and eleven was the age when the pool was for babies. He and Bobby Meek had been out on the point for a while, skipping rocks into the current to see how far they got before the water took them, and she had looked up twice and seen them there, two skinny brown boys against the bright water, and looked back down.

The third time she looked up, Roy was standing at the very end of the point with the brown water going past his feet, and she felt it.

She had no name for it. It was not a thought. It was in her stomach first, and then it was in her hands, a wanting to get up that her body had before her mind did. She was on her feet without deciding to be, with the book still open in her hand.

"Roy."

He looked back at her.

"Don't."

He grinned. He was going to say something. She could see him getting ready to say it, the thing about babies and the pool, with Bobby beside him waiting to laugh. And then, because he was eleven and she was his sister and she had said don't to him a thousand times about a thousand things, she felt the thing in her hands go slack. She thought, he's a strong swimmer, he's stronger than I am, he's only showing off for Bobby, he's stood on that point a hundred times. She sat back down against the log and picked up the book and found her place, and read the same line three times before it went in.

She heard Bobby before she heard anything else. Bobby was not shouting as boys shout in water. He was shouting one word, over and over, and it was Roy's name.

She was in the river before she knew she had dropped the book. The pool side, because that was where she was. The mud took her feet and let them go. Then around the point, and the current took her legs out from under her. She had been told about it all her life and she had never been in it, and in one second she knew what it was: not water you swam in, but a thing that took you, all of you at once, faster than anybody could move, and did not care. It swung her in along the Kentucky bank just below the point, where the channel came back against the shore and had cut it under, and she got a hand on a root hanging out of the cut bank and held it. The water pulled at her. She looked downstream.

There was nothing to see. There was the water going by, brown, fast, with the sun on it so that it hurt to look, and Bobby on the point screaming with his hands at his sides, and Frank behind her in the pool standing up to his waist with his mouth open, and there was nothing else. She hung on the root and looked as far downstream as she could see, to where the river went behind the trees at the next bend, and there was nothing in it but water.

She held onto the root. The current had her from the waist down and wanted the rest of her. The root was slick, and she got her other hand on it and then a knee against the cut bank, and hung there with the water pulling at her dress, and something in the tangle under the bank, a broken branch the river had jammed in the roots, drove into her ribs under the arm every time the current swung her, and she could not get off it and could not let go to try.

Behind her Bobby's screaming stopped, and she turned her head in time to see him go, up the path into the willows at a run, and the two Sutton boys scrambling out of the pool after him, and then there was nobody on the bar but Frank.

He was standing to his waist in the pool where she had left him, across the low sand of the point from her, close enough to see her face, all by himself, where the Sutton boys had left him. He was not moving. His arms were down at his sides in the water and his face was turned toward the point where Roy had been, and he was calling Roy's name, over and over, in the voice he used when he was lost in a store, and when Roy did not answer he called hers, Nell, Nell, and she could not answer him, because she did not have the breath. She watched him understand that nobody was going to answer. She saw his face change.

And then he started toward her, wading, toward the point and the place where the pool ended and the current began, because she was the only one of them he could still see, and she got a breath from somewhere and screamed at him, "Frank! No! Go back!" in a voice she did not know she had, and he stopped, and went back, two steps, three, until the water was at his waist again, and stood there with his arms down in the water and did not come on, and called for Mama. She would remember that for the rest of her life. Not the water. Frank, eight years old, standing alone in the pool where she had sent him back to, calling for his mother.

She hung there. She could not have said how long. Her arms began to go. She had time to think that she was going to let go, and that she did not want to, and that it would not matter what she wanted. Then there were boots on the bank above her and a man's voice saying her name, and Mr. Sutton came down the bank on his knees and got her under the arms and hauled her up out of the water onto the mud.

Bobby had run. He ran up the path through the willows, screaming, and the Sutton boys ran after him. Mr. Sutton had been at the landing patching a johnboat when the three of them came out of the willows onto the road. He heard the screaming and dropped what he had in his hands and ran to meet them. His boys hit him at a run and hung onto him, one on each side, with their faces in his shirt, crying, and did not try to say anything. It was Bobby who told him. Bobby came up behind them shaking, with his face wet, and got it out in pieces, because Roy was his friend and he had run a quarter mile to say it: Roy had gone in off the point. Nell had gone in after him. That was all Bobby had.

Mr. Sutton looked at him and said, "Where's the little one? Where's Frank?" and Bobby said, "In the pool." Mr. Sutton got his boys off him. He told Bobby to run on up Water Street for the men, and his older boy to run for Nell's mother, and the little one to go home to his, and when the three of them had gone he turned for the willows.

He came down the path at a run, expecting, she understood later, to find two of them gone. He came out of the willows onto the sand and saw Frank standing alone in the pool. Frank did not say anything. He lifted one arm out of the water and pointed at the point, and Mr. Sutton went around it along the bank not expecting to find anything.

That was how there came to be boots on the bank above her, and a man's voice saying her name. She had been lucky. Very lucky. She heard it said afterward, in the store, at church, in the voices people used when they thought she was out of hearing. Nobody ever said it to her face.

He stood her on her feet on the mud and looked at her face and did not ask her anything. He did not have to. He had grown up on this bar the same as she had, in the same pool, and the reason nobody went off the point was a boy he had known, Arlen Kimbrough, sixteen and the best swimmer on the river. Arlen had gone off the point on a dare one July, with Mr. Sutton, twelve years old, watching from the sand, and the current had taken him, and the men had found him two days later under a drift pile below the bend.

He looked downstream, once, the whole length of what could be seen, and Nell saw his face while he looked, and did not forget it.

He put his arm around her and walked her back around the point along the bank to the sand, and went down into the pool and lifted Frank out of the water and carried him up and set him on his feet next to her, and Frank went into her and she had her arms around him before she knew she had moved them.

Then he put a hand on each of Nell's shoulders, with Frank still held between them, and turned her until she had to look at him over Frank's head, and waited until she did, and said, "You hold onto him. Right here. You do not move from this spot, either one of you, until somebody comes. You hear me?" and did not let go of her shoulders until she nodded.

He went out onto the point, to the very end of it where Roy had stood, and put his hands around his mouth and called Roy's name down the river, Roy, Roy Hale, in a voice that carried to the bend. Then he went down the bank below the point, along the water, calling as he went, looking into every eddy and every drift pile and every place the current set a thing against the shore, as far down as he could go and still see the two of them standing on the sand behind him, and stood there at the edge of the trees a long time looking at the bend where the river went out of sight, and called once more. Nothing came back but the water.

He knew, better than anybody in Calder, that one man on foot could not get a boy back from that current. He had looked anyway. Then he came back up the bank toward them, and halfway up he stopped and lifted his head, listening, and Nell heard it too, up in the willows, faint, a voice calling her name and Frank's. "Your mother's coming," he said.

Her mother had heard a boy go past the kitchen window. He had run out of screaming by then, a quarter mile up from the bar, and what went past the window was a boy crying and calling for somebody, anybody, in a voice that had gone to pieces, and she went to the window and saw that it was Bobby Meek, who had gone down to the bar with her boys, coming up from the bar without them. That was all she needed. All three of hers were down there.

She set the colander in the sink and went out the back door without drying her hands, and she was calling their names before she was off the porch, Roy and Nell and Frank, all three, over and over, in case they were in the yard, in case they were on the road, in case one of them would answer and it would be all right. Nobody answered. The Sutton boy sent to fetch her met her on Water Street coming the other way at a run, and she caught him by both arms and held him and said, "What happened. Tell me what happened," and could not make herself ask the other thing, and the boy said it for her: "Roy went in off the point. Nell went in after him." She let go of him and ran, and he turned around and ran back down behind her.

Nell had not moved. She stood on the sand with her arms around Frank and Frank's face in her wet dress, both of them shaking, and watched Mr. Sutton's back go down the bank and stop at the trees and come back, and could think of nothing but the water, and whether it would give him back, and could not think past that.

The voice in the willows came closer, calling for her and Frank, and it was her mother's, and it was a voice Nell had never heard come out of her mother. Nell took Frank by the hand and went toward it. She did not decide to. She had nodded to Mr. Sutton and meant it, and she went anyway, up the sand toward the willows with Frank stumbling along behind her.

Her mother came out of the willows onto the bar running and saw them coming, and got to them, and got both of them, one arm around each, hard, wet apron and wet children, for about as long as it takes to say it. Then she held Nell off by the shoulders and looked at her face, and looked at Frank's, and said, "Stay here. You stay right here," and let go of them and went past them at a run for the point, calling Roy.

Mr. Sutton saw her coming and came up the last of the bank at a run and met her at the water's edge, with the current going by a step behind him, and got his arms around her, and held on.

She fought him. Nell had never seen her mother fight anything. She twisted in his arms and beat on his forearms with her wet fists and screamed Roy's name at the water, over and over, and tried to get her feet under her to go in, and Mr. Sutton, who had held onto a lot of things in his life, held onto her, and said nothing, and let her hit him. It went on until it stopped. Her mother's voice gave out before her arms did, and then her arms did, and she stood in his arms with her wet hands hanging at her sides, looking downstream, and Mr. Sutton felt her stop and let her go, and she stayed where she was.

After that she did not make a sound, and did not move, and was still standing there when the men came.

The men came with the boat from the landing, Bobby's father and four others carrying it between them, and put it in above the point, and Mr. Sutton left her mother standing where she was and went down the bank and got in with them, and they went down the river, and two more went down along the banks on foot, one on either side. Nell sat on the sand in her wet dress with Frank against her side, both of them shaking, and Mrs. Meek, who had come down behind the men, put a towel around her and said things. Nell did not remember much of what she said and did not try to. She remembered the boat going around the bend and out of sight. She remembered her mother's back.

They found Roy the next morning, a mile down, against a snag. She did not see him. Her father went. Her father had come in off the railroad in the night, somebody had got word to him at the yard, and he had walked in the house and looked at her mother and gone out again with the men. He came back in the morning and sat on the porch with his boots on and his hands between his knees. He did not take the boots off. He did not speak. Her mother brought him coffee, and he did not drink it.

The funeral was at the Baptist church with the casket closed. The whole of Water Street came, and Front Street, and men from the railroad in their good suits standing at the back with their hats. Nell sat between her mother and Frank in the front pew and looked at the casket and could not make it be Roy. Her mother's hands lay flat on her lap the whole service, pressed there, and did not move, and Nell, who knew those hands, knew what they were holding down, and could not help her. Bobby Meek was there with his parents, white as paper, and would not look at her. Somebody had told him not to, or he had told himself.

Her mother never said a word to her about it. Not that night, not at the funeral, not after. Not once, in all the years. She did not say it was Nell's fault and she did not say it wasn't. She did not ask what Nell had seen or where she had been sitting or whether she had said anything to him.

She went on cooking and washing. The beans were still on the table the day it happened, half of them in the jars and the rest in the colander in the sink where she had set it down, and beans do not keep. The morning they found him she went into the kitchen and finished them, every jar, because they would have spoiled otherwise and because it was something to do with her hands, and put them in the cellar. They ate them that winter, and nobody at the table said a word about which beans they were.

When she talked to Nell at all, which was no more than she ever had, it was about school, and church, and Frank. About the river she said nothing, and about Roy she said nothing, and Nell understood, at fourteen, that the not saying was going to go on for the rest of their lives.

Frank started sleeping in her room. He did not ask. He came in the first night with his pillow and got in the other side of her bed, and she let him, and he did it for a year.

What she said to herself, in bed, with Frank asleep against her back and the room across the hall empty now with the door shut, was that she had felt it. She had felt it and she had got up and she had said the word, and then she had sat back down. She went over it and over it. She could not decide whether the feeling had been real or whether she was making it up afterward, out of what happened, so that she could have something to blame. She thought probably she was making it up. Big sisters had feelings about little brothers on riverbanks every day of the world, and nothing happened, and they forgot. She had had a feeling and something had happened and so she remembered it. That was all it was. That was all it could be.

She told herself that for a long time. She half believed it. What she could not do afterward, whether she believed it or not, was walk away from a worry once she had it. She would go back and look again. She would stand where she was a minute longer than she needed to before she left. She would get up, not knowing what she meant to do once she was up, and stand there feeling foolish, and could not have said whether that was sense or only Roy, and it did not matter which, because she could not make herself sit back down either way. She stayed up anyway.
