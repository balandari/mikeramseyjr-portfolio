# Chapter One — Bangladesh

*A cholera-relief camp outside Dacca · Spring 1972*

The heat inside the tent was almost unbearable. It came off the canvas overhead. It came off the packed earth. It lay in the aisle between the two rows of cots, like something you had to wade through. It was late afternoon, and Nell had stopped noticing the smell around the second day, lime and chlorine and the other thing the lime and chlorine were meant to cover up, but she had never stopped noticing the heat. Twenty-two cots, eleven on each side, each with a round hole cut in the canvas and a galvanized bucket underneath, because a cholera patient could not get up and would not stop, and the only mercy the tent could offer was that he did not have to lie in it. Eighteen of the cots were full. The two aides, Shefali and her cousin whose name Nell still could not keep straight, went up and down the rows with the buckets and the lime. At the far end, Dolores from Owensboro, fifty-three and the only other American in the camp who could put a needle in a vein, was singing under her breath to a woman who had stopped answering to her name that morning.

Nell had her back to the flap. She was changing the glass bottle of fluid that ran through a rubber tube into the arm of a boy of about ten named Montu, who had come in around midday barely conscious, carried in by his mother across four miles of embankment road with his head hanging over her shoulder. Three bottles later he was asking for rice. His mother sat on the ground at the head of the cot with her sari pulled over her hair and would not be moved. Nell had stopped trying. She got the empty bottle off its nail, hooked the full one on by the wire loop at its base, watched the drip start in the little glass chamber, and counted it down to the rate she wanted with her father's watch. It was a nurse's watch with a sweep second hand. He had bought it for her in Louisville the week she graduated, and it had been to Da Nang and back and had never once stopped.

She heard the canvas before she saw anything. The flap at the far end had been thrown back hard enough to bring the pole with it.

Kabir stood in the opening with the light behind him. She knew him at once, even against the glare. The committee's driver had gone out the gate early that morning at the wheel of the Land Rover with the American beside him and a list of things to buy in the city, and by rights he should still have been there, standing in the pharmacy on Mitford Road arguing over the price of tetracycline. Instead he was in her tent, breathing hard, and there was determination in his face. Mud caked his legs to the thigh and streaked one side of him to the shoulder, the kind of mud a man does not pick up on his feet. His shirt was torn at the shoulder. He held a folded cloth against his hairline, and blood had come through it. His left forearm lay across his stomach, and the hand at the end was swollen and turned in at the wrist, the fingers curled.

"Sister." He had never called her anything else. "There has been an accident. The Land Rover went off the road and over. The sahib is hurt. His chest. They have him in a truck at the end of the tents."

Montu's mother had turned to look. Dolores had stopped singing.

His chest. That was the whole of it, as far as Nell was concerned. The tents were fluid and Dolores could handle it. A chest was minutes. Rahman could not walk away from forty patients, and she had carried chests before, in the air, with nothing but what was in the bag.

Nell made sure of Montu's drip and had her bag, the one with the Air Force stencil half worn off it, ready to go. She had carried it out of the service and into every place since, because it was the right size and she knew where everything in it was in the dark.

"Dolores. Montu's bottle is fresh. The woman on eleven wants watching."

"Go," Dolores said.

Kabir reached with his good hand into his breast pocket while he talked and came out with a folded paper, the morning's list, soft and brown with paddy water. He was holding it out to nobody in particular.

Dr. Rahman was already coming across the trodden ground from the men's tent, drying his hands on a towel; he had heard Kabir through the canvas, or heard the flap, or heard the sound a camp makes when something has happened in it. Not thinking, he dropped the towel over the guy rope, having passed by it so many times before.

"Kabir's hurt too," she said.

"I can see that." Rahman took the list from Kabir and slipped it into his pocket. He supported Kabir by the uninjured elbow and guided him toward the narrow shade beside the water cart. "Come, bhai. Sit. You can tell me the rest once you're off your feet."

Nell headed down the row toward the lorry, the bag banging against her hip with the long, fast stride she had learned on flight lines.

---

She passed the bare place between the two big tents where the Land Rover once stood, and the men's tent, where Rahman had come from. Behind them she passed the cook tent and the store tent with its padlock. The water cart sat past those, a tank on two wheels with a spigot that a boy filled from the government tanker every second day. The burn pit lay downwind of all of it, and she caught the smell of it as she went by.

She had gone down this row more times than she could have counted and had never once looked at any of it. Now every guy rope and bucket, the padlock, the smoke, stood out sharp at the edges. Things went clear like that when someone was in trouble ahead of you. She had learned not to trust the clearness, only to use it.

The one thing out here she had looked at, and looked at every day, stood past the last tent: three bamboo poles on the raised patch of ground that had been somebody's threshing floor before the war, standing up against the sky where the well was going in. That was the well that was meant to put clean water within reach of the tents so that fewer people arrived in them. She had walked out there most evenings since the crew came, when the tents were quiet enough to leave, to stand at the edge of the threshing floor and ask Rafiq, the foreman, how long. He was a small, exact man who had been sinking wells, he told her once, since he was younger than Kabir, and he gave every question, hers included, as few words as it would take. *Ninety feet*, he had told her the night before. *Tomorrow we wash. Then the screen. Then the pump.* He had never once introduced her to the American, who was always off on the far side of the floor with his hands in his back pockets, looking at the ground, or at the pipe, or at the sky over the field, anywhere, she had noticed, but at the people. She had never asked to be.

It could not have taken her a minute to get there, and it felt longer. She had seen the whole camp and thought about Dolores and the woman on eleven and the well and Rafiq, and all of it had fit inside the time it took to walk past a few tents. She knew that about emergencies too. They stretched.

The truck was where Kabir had said: a grain lorry with its tailgate down, backed to the end of the row where the ground was hardest. It was an old Bedford with a Bengali name painted across the front bumper in white and a string of tin flowers wired to the mirror strut. It was half loaded with jute sacks of rice, stacked forward against the cab wall, and in the open half of the bed, behind the sacks, the men had laid a plank flat on the floor and laid the American on it, with somebody's folded shirt under his head. A man she didn't know stood at the tailgate with his arms folded and a checked cloth over his shoulder. That was the driver, Motaleb, who had been driving that road since the British left. Another, younger, sat in the cab with the door open and one bare foot on the step, and that was his mate.

She had seen the American across the camp for weeks, a big man, sunburnt red across the back of the neck, always at the far end where the well was going in, always beside Rafiq, never once in the tents. She had never been closer to him than the width of the threshing floor and had never heard his voice. Now she put her knee on the tailgate and climbed up with the bag.

He was awake. That was the first thing she looked for, and she was glad of it; it wasn't much, but it was something. His face was gray under the sunburn, the color the blood leaves behind when it goes somewhere else, and wet with a sweat that had nothing to do with the heat. He was breathing in short quick bites that stopped high in the chest. His right arm was clamped across his ribs. His eyes found her and stayed, a hurt man's eyes fixing on the one person in reach who seemed to know what to do.

"Don't talk yet," she said. "I'm a nurse. I'm going to look you over. Kabir says the car went over."

"Twice," he said, on one of the short breaths, and then nothing.

She started at the head, because that was the order, and the order was what you had when you had nothing else. Scalp: a scrape above the ear, the hair matted with dried mud, no bleeding worth a dressing. Pupils: she shaded his eyes with her hand and took it away, and they shrank as they should, and for half a second, looking into them that close, something in her stopped. Not a thought, nothing she could have named, a catch, like a foot finding a step it didn't expect. Then it was gone and she was a nurse checking pupils again. Even. He knew where he was and what day it was and that he'd been on his way to Dacca, and he told her his name was Gil Hanratty, and she said "All right, Mr. Hanratty," and went on. Neck: she ran her fingers down the bones one at a time and pressed each one, watching his face. He didn't flinch. That was no proof the neck was sound. She had seen men walk around with broken necks. But a flinch would have told her worse, and she took what she could. Then the chest.

She lifted his right arm away from his ribs. He made a sound through his teeth.

"I know," she said, and laid her hand flat on the right side of his chest.

His shirt was khaki and soaked through, and there was a notebook in the breast pocket, a small one, the kind with a pencil in the spine. She undid the buttons and moved the shirt aside. The right side of his chest was already fuller than the left, and the color was coming up under the skin, a dark red stain going purple at its edges, spreading from the armpit down toward the belt. When he breathed in she felt what she'd been waiting to feel: a grating under her palm, bone against bone, and that whole side of him lagging, a half-beat behind the rest.

She got the stethoscope out of the bag, the same old brass-and-rubber one, taped at the fork, and listened. Left side, loud, clear. Right side: something, far off, like a man breathing in the next room. Then she laid the middle finger of her left hand flat on his chest and tapped it with the middle finger of her right and listened to the note. Above the nipple it rang hollow. Below it, the note went flat. She marked the place with her thumbnail, a faint scratch in the sweat. It was a crude way to mark a line and she knew it, but no pen was going to write on that skin.

Belly soft. Pelvis firm when she pressed the crests inward. Legs straight, no flinch when she pressed the shins and the knees; she couldn't get at his feet through the work boots, so she asked him to move them, and both boots moved. Then the cuff, out of the bottom of the bag, wound on his left arm above the elbow; she pumped it and let it down with the stethoscope in the crook, and got it on the first try, because the lorry's engine was off: one-ten over seventy. Not bad. Not what it would be.

His hands she did last, both of them, turning them over, because a man in a rollover throws his hands out. They were skinned across the knuckles and otherwise whole, big square hands with old healed cuts on them and a thin gold band on the left. She held the right one a moment longer than she had any reason to. It was the same catch as at his eyes, there and gone. She put it down on the plank.

"Ribs," she said. "Right side, several. There's blood in there, or air, or both. It isn't going to get better in this truck."

"How bad."

"I don't know yet. I'll know more as the hours go, and I'd rather learn it closer to Dacca."

Kabir came back down the row with his forehead taped and his wrist splinted between two strips of cardboard cut from a Ringer's carton and a roll of gauze. Rahman's work, fast and square. Motaleb started talking, low and steady, and Kabir put it into English in pieces.

The road shoulder had gone under the outside wheels six miles out, where the embankment ran between two flooded fields. Soft; it was April; nobody had re-cut the shoulders since before the war. Over twice, into the paddy on the low side. The Land Rover had come to rest on its roof in two feet of water with its wheels still spinning. Kabir had come out the driver's door. The sahib had not come out of anything; Kabir and the lorry men had lifted him out through the window, and he had sworn then, at length, in a voice Kabir had not heard from him before, and that was how Kabir had known he was alive. The lorry had been a quarter mile behind. The lorry men had wanted to drive straight through to Dacca with him. Kabir had said no. The camp was nearer and the nurses were at the camp, and he had made them turn round.

She looked at him, twenty-two, his own arm broken, his good shirt ruined, and thought of him standing in a wet field an hour ago making that call and making it stick against two grown men who wanted to go the other way.

"Good," she said.

"I didn't know what else to do." He was looking at the truck bed, not at her. "He couldn't breathe. I thought, the nurses."

"You were right."

She did not know that he was. Turning back had cost an hour of road, and the road was the one thing she could not give this man. Without the turn, though, he would be on that plank alone, flat, with no line in him and nobody counting, and she did not know what that would have cost him either. It was the kind of sum she had stopped trying to do out loud.

---

Rahman came up the tailgate as he went up everything, left foot first, the right brought up after it. He was a small, precise man in his forties who had run the tents with two aides for a month before the first relief nurses came, sleeping on a cot in the store tent with the drug ledger under his pillow. The right knee had been broken in a bicycle accident in his student days in Dacca and had never entirely forgiven him; when it stopped him on a step he called it his elder and waited for it, and it was the only thing in the camp anyone had heard him make a joke about. He got into the bed, shifted one sack aside with his foot to make room, and knelt on the far side of the plank from her with the bad leg out straight along the grain. Then he did what she had done, in the same order, without saying so: the head, the neck, the chest, both sides with his own stethoscope, the tapping, the throat. He put two fingers in the notch between the collarbones and held them there while Gil breathed. The windpipe ran under his fingers there. If air built up in the chest enough to push things over, the windpipe would be the first thing to move.

"Middle, for now," he said.

"Yes."

"Veins flat, for now." He sat back on his good heel and looked at her, not at the patient. "It looks like blood more than air. That can change, and you may not see it change until it has. You know what I've got. Ringer's, needles, gauze, aspirin, tetracycline for the tents. No blood to give him. No morphine. And nothing to drain a chest with: no tube, no drain. Whatever's gathering in there stays in there until a surgeon takes it out."

He said it evenly, like a man laying out the contents of his pockets. Then he leaned forward and put two fingers back in the notch of the collarbones, and held them there through three of the short breaths, and took them away.

"If it turns to air out there, if the pressure builds and he can't get any breath at all, the one thing that helps is a needle put through the chest wall to let the air out. You've seen it done. It's a doctor's job, and it can go wrong, and I'm not telling you to do it. I'm telling you so you'll know what you're looking at if it comes. A surgeon stops this. He goes to one tonight, and you go with him, because you've carried worse at thirty thousand feet, and I've forty people in those tents."

"Yes."

"The Medical College, not Holy Family. I want a surgeon who's awake." He was reaching back for the second bottle, which Dolores had come down the row carrying, along with a folded blanket and a hurricane lantern with the glass smoked black on one side. "Keep him sitting up if you can. If his pressure goes, you'll want him lower and he'll want to sit up. You'll have to judge it. There's no right answer with what you've got. Keep the line open. Keep him talking. A word here and there. I want you to know he's still with you."

She had run the line while Kabir was talking: a needle in the back of his left hand, taped down flat, and a rubber tube up to a glass bottle of Ringer's, water and dissolved salts, the same fluid she had been hanging on cholera patients all week, hooked upside down by its wire loop on a nail in the slatted side of the lorry. She had set the drip slow. Not for blood yet; for the road. Between them they got him half-sitting against the back wall of the cab, with the plank behind his back and a sack of grain under his knees, and Dolores's blanket over his legs against the night that was coming. Then they strapped his right arm across the injured side with a lungi, the wraparound cloth the men wore from waist to ankle, so the ribs had something to lean on. This one was borrowed off the mate, who stood in the road in a pair of shorts and did not seem to mind. He didn't help and didn't fight. When they had him settled he let his head go back against the cab and shut his eyes. He opened them again at once. She saw that he didn't like the dark.

The notebook had come out of his pocket when she moved the shirt, and lay on the sacks. He saw her see it.

"Bag," he said. "Put it in. Your bag."

She did. It was warm and damp and had a rubber band round it, and she didn't open it.

Rahman got up off the sacks, the good leg first, a hand on the cab wall for the other, and went to the tailgate and looked down at Kabir, who was standing beside it with his splinted arm held against his chest.

"You stay. That wrist wants plaster."

"I was driving him." Kabir said it as a fact, not a plea. "I know where the road went this morning. I'll ride in the cab."

Rahman looked at the wrist, and at Kabir, and let it go. "Keep it higher than your heart."

He sat on the edge of the tailgate with his legs hanging, looked at the drop, and let himself down with one hand on the truck's side, the left foot down first, the right brought after it, the same order as always, feeling for the ground before he gave the elder his weight. He straightened, lifted the tailgate with both hands, and stood on his toes to shoot the pins home, first the left, then the right. He slapped the steel twice. To Motaleb, in Bengali, something short that made the driver nod and throw the cloth from his shoulder onto the seat; to Dolores, "The woman on eleven"; to Nell, over the tailgate, "Send the lorry back tonight." Then he walked up the row of tents without turning round, because there were forty people in them, and Dolores went after him with the empty bottle carton, and the mate hung the lantern on a nail at the front of the bed where it would swing but not fall, and climbed into the cab.

Then it was the two of them in the bed of the lorry among the sacks, and up front the driver, his mate in the middle of the bench, and Kabir at the near window with his splinted arm braced across his lap, which was not higher than his heart, and she let that go too.

---

She sat on a sack at Gil's right side with her hand flat on his chest, over the strapping, so she could feel the ribs rise. She watched the second hand of her father's watch and counted the rises for thirty seconds. Fourteen. Twenty-eight a minute, then: fast for a man at rest, about what she'd expect. Then two fingers on the inside of his left wrist, above the tape, and a full minute this time, because she wanted the number exact to measure the rest of the night against. A hundred and four. Fast. Full enough.

She wrote both on the back of her hand with the pen from the bag, and the time, and *110/70* under them.

"Where's home?" she said. Rahman had told her to keep him talking, and it was the first thing that came.

"Ohio." A breath. "Pomeroy. On the river."

"Kentucky. I grew up on the river too. Other side, a good ways down."

He turned his head a little on the folded shirt to look at her, and something that was nearly a smile moved the gray face. "You don't say."

"Tell me about yours," she said. "A piece at a time. Don't spend all your air on it at once."

The lorry backed, turned in its own length with the driver's arm out the window, and found the embankment road. Through the slats she watched the camp fall back, the two big tents with the light going gold on them, the water cart, the burn pit with its thread of smoke, and then, on the raised ground beyond the last tent, the three bamboo poles of the well. Word had only just reached the threshing floor; she could see it in how they stood. The crew were in a row under the poles where somebody had called them off the lever, and Rafiq was already coming down off the mound toward the road at a run, and then, when he saw the lorry was not going to stop, he stopped, at the edge of the road, with his hands at his sides. Gil had turned his head to look. He pulled in a breath to call across to him, and it stopped high in his chest where all his breaths had been stopping, and he let it out and got his left hand up off the sack a few inches instead, and held it there. Rafiq lifted one hand back. Then the road bent and the poles went behind a stand of banana trees and were gone, and whatever Gil had meant to call to him went with them.

The road ran straight and narrow on its bank between the flooded fields, one lorry wide with soft ground on both sides, and Motaleb took it at a walking pace for the first mile, reading the shoulders. The evening water showed him what the morning light had not shown Kabir: where the bank had bled out into the paddy, the water lay a shade darker. The fields went from the color of weak tea to the color of pewter as the light left them, and a line of white egrets went over, low, all in the same direction.

At the first village the lamps were being lit, kerosene wicks in the open fronts of the shops, one bare electric bulb over the tea stall on a wire from nowhere she could see. Somebody's radio was playing film songs. From the little tin-roofed mosque at the far end the evening call to prayer went up over the water, unhurried, and men were coming out of the houses toward it with their lungis hitched. A child ran alongside the lorry for a hundred yards, shouting something friendly, and dropped back. She counted again when they were through: thirty. A hundred and ten.

Past the village the road bent, and Motaleb slowed for a place where the bank had slumped and water lay across the crown of the road in a shallow sheet. He put the lorry into it at a crawl. She felt the bed tilt toward the low side and felt Gil's whole body go rigid under her hand, and put her other hand behind his head. When the lorry came level he let go all at once and made a sound and said, "Sorry."

"Don't be. Breathe out. Slow. Now in. That's enough. You don't need all of it."

"Feels like I can't get to the bottom of it."

"You can't. Not on that side. Take what the other one gives you."

He tried it. She watched him try it. After a while he said, without opening his eyes, "Did they get the screen in."

It took her a second. "The well? I don't know. They were still on the lever when we went by."

He was quiet a moment. "Good sand at ninety feet. I saw the samples." Breath. "It's in the book. Tell him the screen goes in the top of the coarse. Not the bottom. He knows. Tell him anyway."

"You can tell him."

He didn't answer that.

---

The last of the light went out of the fields. For a few minutes the water on either side of the road held a gray shine, and then it didn't, and there was only the road, and the lantern swinging on its nail, and the two of them in its circle. The lorry had one headlamp, the left. The right had gone the week before on another embankment road, when Motaleb, meeting a jute lorry on a bank with no room for two, had chosen the soft edge over the other lorry's side and given up the lamp to the encounter; he had not yet found the money for a new one, or a new one to buy, and he had been driving these roads by one lamp and his own memory of them for seven nights. So what lay ahead was a wedge of light on the near shoulder and nothing on the far one. He took it slower. Up front the cab's rear window slid open, and Kabir's arm came out the side window with the torch in his good hand, held out and down, sweeping the edge they couldn't see. She heard him say something low to the driver, and the driver answer without turning his head.

She counted. Thirty-four. A hundred and eighteen.

She got the cuff on him again and tried the stethoscope and could hear nothing at all over the engine and the road. So she did it the other way, as she had learned to in a C-141 with four engines going: pumped the cuff up, put two fingers on the wrist, and let the air out slowly until the pulse came back under them. Ninety-something. She had had one-ten at the tailgate.

She went back to the chest with her fingers and tapped, and the flat note seemed higher than it had been. Seemed, in a moving truck, with a finger. She knew better than to trust it to the width of anything. But it seemed higher. She put two fingers in the notch of his collarbones as Rahman had: still in the middle, as far as she could tell by the lantern. The veins she couldn't see. It told her less than she wanted. Blood, most likely. Or blood and air both. Either way, nothing in this truck could reach it.

She got up on her knees, braced a hand on the cab roof against the sway, and rapped on the open rear window. Kabir's face turned in the glow of the dash.

"Can he go any faster?"

Kabir spoke to the driver. Motaleb answered, still not turning, three or four words.

"He says the road is the speed," Kabir said, and then, on his own account, "Where it lets him, he will."

She sat back down. At the next firm stretch the engine note climbed and the sacks shifted under her, and she felt it, and was grateful, and did not ask again.

A mile on, the road told her what he had meant. The lorry stopped. The mate got down with the torch and walked ahead into the dark, and she got up on her knees on the sacks, one hand still on Gil's chest, to see over the cab: a culvert where the road crossed a channel between two fields, and the concrete deck of it gone, blown or washed out or both sometime in the last year, and nobody had come to put it back. Somebody had laid two lines of planks across the gap on the stubs of the old beams, one line for each wheel, and the mate walked them first with the torch, testing with his foot, and then stood on the far side and held the light on the left-hand planks while Motaleb put the front wheels onto them by inches. She felt the bed drop a hand's breadth as the wheels found the planks, and rock, and go on, and drop again at the back, and then the tires were on the bank again and the mate was climbing back into the cab. It had taken longer than it should have. She had not breathed much in any of it. Gil had held as still as a man can who is breathing thirty times a minute, and had not looked away from her face the whole time.

"I'm thirsty," he said.

That was the blood talking. She knew it from the flights and from the tents both, the body asking for what it had lost, in the only word it had. She reached up and opened the clamp on the line all the way and watched the drip turn to a stream in the little chamber. The first bottle was two-thirds gone. There was the second, and that was all.

"You can have a wet cloth. Not a drink. Not with your chest like that."

"Why."

"Because a surgeon may want you empty." She wet the corner of a gauze from her canteen and put it to his mouth. He pulled at it, sucking the wet out of the cloth, and would have taken more. She saw his hands, both of them, lying open on the sacks, and that the right one had begun to shake.

She took his wrist again. Fifteen seconds this time, times four, because she didn't want to wait a whole minute for the number. Thirty-one. A hundred and twenty-four. Thready. She could feel it, but she had to hunt for it, and the skin over it was cool and damp where an hour ago it had been hot. She tried the cuff once more, by feel: the pulse came back under her fingers at something in the eighties, and then she let the air all the way out and sat with her hand on his arm.

Here was the judgment Rahman had left her. His pulse was running away with less and less to run on; she had seen men faint sitting up when the blood got low, and go down hard. Lower would help his heart. But the blood in his chest was pressing on the lung, and the lower he lay the harder that lung would have to work for what little it was giving him. She had no idea where the best place was between the two. There was only somewhere in between. She dragged the sack out from under his knees and pushed it in behind his shoulders instead, so that he lay back against it, lower than before but not flat, and jammed another sack against his feet so he wouldn't slide down, and pulled the blanket back up. Then she put her hand back on his chest.

"No." He got a hand on her arm. There was strength in it still. "Up. I need up."

"I know. It's going to feel worse lying back. Your heart's working harder than it can keep up with, and I need what blood you've got going where it counts. Halfway. Stay with me here."

"I can't get."

"You're getting some. Not enough, and I know it, but some. Look at me. Look at me and breathe out."

He looked at her. In the lantern light his eyes were the only part of him that wasn't gray. He breathed out.

The lorry crawled. The wedge of light went ahead of them over the wet crown of the road. The far side of the bank was nothing at all. She heard Kabir call something and felt the lorry ease left, away from whatever the torch had found, and go on.

She kept him talking. It was the last treatment she had. She asked him about the sludging, and he told her, in pieces, between breaths, with his left hand moving a little on the sack as if it were working something. The hand-valve, the chain, the lever, how the sand came up gray and then the good coarse yellow, how Rafiq could tell the depth by the sound the pipe made in the hole. She asked him about Pomeroy. He told her about the floodwall and the lock. His father had drilled wells with a cable rig. He asked her nothing; he didn't have the air. When he went quiet for longer than a breath she said his name, "Gil," and he came back up to it, slower each time.

A bus went by the other way, all lit up inside and packed to the doors, and its lights swept over the two of them and were gone, and in that second she saw his face plainly for the first time since the tailgate, and did not like what she saw.

Second bottle. She hung it by feel, the wire loop over the nail, and got the drip going. The empty first one she pushed down among the sacks so it wouldn't roll.

There was no hurrying the road. She had known that at the tailgate and she knew it differently now, with his pulse getting away from her two fingers and the flat note that seemed to be climbing his side and a surgeon somewhere ahead in a city whose lights she couldn't see yet. She had sat in this hour before. Not this man, not this truck, but this, the hour where the numbers go one way and the road goes the other and you have done everything there is and you sit and hold a wrist. She had held them at altitude, and she had held them in the tents, and once, when she was fourteen, she had held nothing at all, on a riverbank, because there had been nothing to hold. She knew what it was. She knew what it looked like when it was over.

He turned his head and found her eyes in the lantern light, and held them, and said, very quietly, "Am I."

There it was again, the moment she met his eyes. The catch. A flash and gone. The thing she could not put a finger on.

She didn't make him finish that one either.

"No," she said. It came out clear and flat and ordinary, the voice she used for reading a number off a chart. "You're not going to die today."

And then, almost before the words had cleared her mouth, the question came up behind them. *How could she know that?* She had no right to it. She had watched a boy her brother's age, burned over half his body, die slowly of it on the flight over the Pacific with her hand on his forehead, and she had promised him nothing, because she'd known better. She had watched nine people in the tents that week go from thirsty to dead in a night. She knew what a pulse of a hundred and twenty-four and climbing was. She knew what a flatness rising in a chest was. She had never in her life said that sentence to a patient, and she had been right not to, and now she'd said it, in a grain lorry, in the dark, to a man she had met that afternoon, as if it were a fact.

She waited for it to go. For the sense of it to drain out, the way a wrong number does when you look at it twice.

It didn't go.

It sat in her, wordless and heavy, the most certain thing she had ever felt, and she could not account for it. Not hope. Nothing so soft. It was the plain, stubborn, unjustified knowledge that this particular man, tonight, was not going to die, and she could no more have said where it came from than she could have said why she'd held his hand a second too long at the tailgate.

*I don't know how I know that,* she thought. *I only know it.*

It frightened her more than his pulse did.

She put her fingers back on his wrist, because that was what she could do, and counted. She didn't say the number to herself this time. She kept her other hand on his chest and felt the bad side lag and the good side work, and the lorry crawled on between the black fields with Kabir's torch sweeping the edge, and somewhere ahead was a city, and a surgeon who was either awake or was not.
