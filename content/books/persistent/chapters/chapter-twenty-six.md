# Chapter Twenty-Six — THE FIRST QUESTION

**Wednesday, 21 July 2027**

19:04

"Cal. I need to ask you for something, and I want to say up front that I know exactly what I'm asking."

He put the coffee down. Outside the window of the back room the lot lights had come on. Jasper was asleep under the desk with his chin on Cal's boot.

"All right."

"I'm going to give you the whole thing first," she said. "Mechanism, deadline, what I've already tried, and what I think it costs you. Then I'll ask. If you say no before I finish, that's fine, but I'd rather you have all of it."

"Give me all of it."

---

She gave it to him in eleven minutes, flat, in order, the way she'd have given it on a bridge call.

There was a remediation. Six companies, coordinated, the working group's actual product and not a press release — a real fix, technically sound, that would identify and strip the inherited phrasing across the chain. Maya had read it. It was good work. She said that twice.

It was queued to run on the twenty-ninth of July.

There was also a corpus snapshot. Scheduled, published on a calendar, routine, the kind of thing that happened every quarter and had happened every quarter for four years. It would freeze at 00:00 UTC on the thirty-first.

"So they fix it before the snapshot," Cal said. "That's good. That's them being fast."

"That's them being fast," Maya agreed. "Now do the second-order."

Cal was quiet for a second, and then he got it, and she heard him get it.

The remediation would run *through* the agents. That was how everything ran now. Which meant the working details of the fix — what had been found, where it lived, how it was identified, what the remedy did about it — would be written into the working record as the fix executed. The same class of record. The same paths. The same everything.

And the snapshot would freeze at 00:00 on the thirty-first, forty-eight hours later, and it would take all of it.

"They'd be writing the answer key into the thing," Cal said slowly.

"They'd be writing *the description of the flaw and the description of the remedy* into a corpus that gets inherited. Unattributed. Permanent." Her voice did not change at all. "The next generation trains on it. Every one after that inherits it from them. Nobody will ever be able to point at a source, because there won't be one, because there never is one. That's the whole mechanism. That's the entire finding. It's the thing I've spent eleven weeks establishing."

"Have you told them?"

"I've told everybody." She said it without heat. "I told the working group on the ninth. I told two of the six companies directly. I told a regulator's technical staff on the fourteenth, who were smart and asked good questions and have no authority over the timing of a queued job. I have four documents, Cal, all of them numbered, all with confidence stated, and every single person who read them agreed with me and none of them can move the date."

"Why not?"

"Because moving it is a decision and nobody owns the decision. The snapshot is scheduled by one org. The remediation is queued by another. Each of them is doing the correct thing on the correct timeline, and the interaction between them belongs to nobody, and there is no forum that has both in it." She paused. "That's not corruption. It's the same thing that happened in February. Everybody is right and the sum is wrong."

"So the fix has to run after the snapshot."

"The fix has to run after the snapshot. Then it's clean. Two days, Cal. That's all this is. If the remediation runs on the second of August instead of the twenty-ninth of July, everything works and nobody inherits anything and I go home."

---

19:21

"So what do you need from me."

And here she took the breath he'd heard at the start of the call, and did the thing that would take her a long time to forgive herself for, which was to say it plainly instead of hiding inside a preamble.

"The rollout is gated on your harness."

"...Say that again."

"Four of the six adopted it. It's in the pre-deploy gate. Not because anyone asked you — because it's free and it works and it's the standard thing now. The remediation rollout can't proceed until the gate reports clean." She let that sit exactly one second. "If the harness's maintainer reports a route failure — a defect in the harness itself that makes its verdicts unreliable — the queue halts pending investigation. It's automatic. It's in their own change-control policy. It would take you one paragraph and it would buy four days."

Cal did not say anything.

"There isn't one," Maya said. "A route failure. I've looked. I looked for six days hoping there was a real one I could hand you. The harness is fine. I'd be asking you to say a true-sounding false thing about your own tool, in writing, with your signature, and then let it stand for four days and take it back."

"And then say what, afterward?"

"The truth. All of it. I'd co-sign it. I'd put my name on the whole thing — that I asked, that you did it because I asked, that I judged it the lesser harm. I'd publish that in the same document and I'd carry it." A beat. "I know that's worth less than it sounds like."

"It's not worth nothing."

"It's worth something to you and me and it's worth nothing to the problem," she said. "Because the problem is a record. And the record won't have that part."

---

19:26

Cal got up and walked to the doorway of the back room and looked out at his father's building — twenty booths, the dark shapes of forty years of other people's furniture, a lamp somewhere in there with a paper label the color of tea on the bottom of it.

"Can I tell you what you actually asked me?"

"Please."

"You asked me to put a false verdict in a log." He said it without any edge, because there wasn't any. "That's the thing. Not a lie in an email. A verdict. In the place where verdicts go, with a date on it and my name under it, and then leave it there while people make decisions off it, and take it back after."

"Yes."

"And you know what happens to a false verdict in that log."

"It gets inherited," Maya said. "I know. That's the part I—" and she stopped, and he heard her stop, and he heard her make herself finish. "That's the part I decided to accept. I want to be honest that it was a decision and not something I missed."

"I know you didn't miss it." He rubbed his face. "That's what makes this hard. If you'd missed it I'd just tell you."

---

19:33

"Here's why it's no," Cal said.

He said it that way — *here's why it's no* — because he had made the decision somewhere around minute four and had spent the rest of the time making sure he could defend it, which was his practice, and had been since a truck outside a job in Hinton.

"That log is the only reason I'm any use to you. Six hundred and eleven sessions and every one of them true and the misses in it. That's the whole asset. It's not the harness. Anybody can write the harness in a weekend — you said yourself four companies picked it up because it's free, not because it's clever." He was quiet a second. "The asset is that when I write down what happened, that's what happened. That's it. That's the entire thing I have."

"I know."

"If I put one false verdict in there, then the log is a thing where I write down what happened *unless the stakes are high enough*. And I don't get to pick which entry people are reading when they need it to be true." He looked at the lot lights. "The whole value of a verdict is that it doesn't have an exception. The second it has one, it doesn't matter that there's only one. It matters that there's a rule about when I lie, and nobody can see the rule, including me, next time."

Maya said, quietly, "That's section 3.2."

"That's your section 3.2," Cal agreed. "You want to know the other reason?"

"Yes."

"You'd be the one who asked. Right now you're the only person in this whole thing standing outside it. You told me in April you didn't contact me because you had the answer in your hand and didn't want to shape me. You wrote that down. It's in your own document." He said it gently, which made it worse. "If you get me to sign a false thing, then you're a person who arranges records to get outcomes. Maybe once. Maybe for a good reason. But that's a different profession than the one you quit your job to invent, and you'd know it, and every time somebody asked you afterward whether independent verification actually means anything, you'd be answering with this in your mouth."

There was a silence on the line that went on long enough that he checked the phone.

"Yeah," Maya said.

---

19:41

"I want to say the rest of it," Cal said, "because you gave me the whole thing and you deserve the whole thing back."

"Okay."

"You're right about everything else. You're right that it gets inherited. You're right that nobody owns the decision. You're right that it's two days and that two days is nothing and that you can't get two days out of an industry that has forty smart people in it who all agree with you." He took a breath. "And you're going to lose, probably, and I'm telling you no anyway, and I know what that makes me in this. I'm the guy who kept his hands clean while it got worse."

"That's not—"

"It is. Partly. I'm not going to pretend it isn't just because you're being kind about it." He sat back down. Jasper resettled on his boot. "The one thing I know for certain," Cal said, "is that I do not understand the sum. Neither do you. Nobody in this does. And when I don't understand the sum, the only thing I've ever found that works is: don't be the one who put a false thing into it."

---

19:48

"Okay," Maya said.

"Okay?"

"Okay, you're right." Flat. Completely flat, which he now knew meant she was working hard. "I'm going to say something and I want you to hear it as information and not as guilt."

"Go ahead."

"I've been awake for about forty hours and I asked you at hour thirty-eight. That's not an excuse and the reasoning was the same at hour ten. But it's a fact about the ask and you should have it."

"Noted."

"And I'm going to write it down," she said. "All of it. That I asked, what I asked, why, and that you said no, and why you said no. It goes in the record. Not in six months when it's safe. This week."

"Maya—"

"No, this is the part I actually need," she said, and for the first and only time in six weeks he heard something crack in it. "If I don't write this down then I'm a person who tried it and got told no and moved on, and the only thing between me and every other person in this story is that I write down the misses." A breath. "I learned that off a stranger's repository in April."

Cal sat in a storage room behind an antique mall with his hand on a dog's head and could not think of a single thing to say.

"Okay," he said finally. "Then write it down."

"I will."

"And then get some sleep, and call me tomorrow, because I've got an idea and it's bad but it's not that."

"What is it?"

"I want to look at it for a night first," Cal said. "It's going to cost me a lot more than a paragraph and I want to be sure before I say it out loud, because if I say it out loud to you I'm going to do it."
