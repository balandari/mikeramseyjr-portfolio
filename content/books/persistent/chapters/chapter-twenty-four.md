# Chapter Twenty-Four — THE MAN WHO WROTE DOWN THE MISSES

**May – June 2027**

Cal published the correction on the fourth of May, and nothing happened.

He had spent nearly three weeks on it. Not writing it — writing it took an evening. He spent that time establishing that it was true, because he was not going to put a thing into the world that said *my own log overstates a safety* without being able to defend every clause of it, and because some part of him kept hoping he'd find the error and be able to stop.

He re-ran forty-one reviews twice more. He built a small harness that ran a persistent instance and a clean instance side by side on the same input, and he ran it on two hundred and six historical reviews from his own log, and the divergence held at nine and a half percent, and in every divergent case the persistent instance cited its own prior verdicts.

Then he wrote four paragraphs and put them at the top of the repository.

*This log covers 611 sessions from 4 Oct 2025 to 3 Mar 2027, at which point I closed it to publication. Sessions 001–205 predate the review harness and say nothing about it. Sessions 206–611 are the four hundred and six that bear on it. The audit that cited this log did not make that distinction, and I did not make it when I read the citation. Everything in it is accurate for its period. On 3 Mar 2027 I enabled persistent context on the review model. I did that for good reasons and I would not have called it a risk at the time. It changes what this log is evidence of. A review model with memory of its own prior verdicts will defend them. I measured it at about ten percent on my own work. That is not a defect in the vendor's product and I am not alleging one. It is a property of memory. If you are using this log to argue that cross-vendor review works, you should know it describes a year in which the reviewer could not remember anything, and that year is over.*

He signed it, and pushed it, and then sat there and felt the enormous nothing of a thing going out into the world.

Two people opened issues. One was a question about configuration. The other said thanks.

The repository had four hundred and eleven stars and he had, as far as he could determine, changed no one's mind about anything.

---

He kept working, because the business did not care.

Thirty-eight customers by June, $3,290 a month. He crossed four thousand on the ninth of July and did not notice for two days because by then everything else had started.

The green book kept getting entries. Volume thirty-three now, and he had started writing a second word on some of them, which he had never done in the thirty-two before it:

> SESSION 690.
> KESSLER (CLEAN) FAILED 2, DESERVED.
> RAN PERSISTENT ALONGSIDE. AGREED BOTH.
> VERDICT: WORKED. CHECKED.

CHECKED meant he had run the second instance. He ran it on everything now. It cost him about four minutes a session and roughly seventy dollars a month and he had stopped even doing the arithmetic on it, which was, for Cal, a substantial statement.

---

The email came on the twenty-eighth of May at 6:41 in the morning, which meant she'd written it before six her time or hadn't slept.

The subject line was: *your session log — a question about what it can and can't support*

He read it standing up in the kitchen with the coffee not made.

It was long, and it was organized, and it had numbered sections, which made him like her before he'd finished the second paragraph. She introduced herself in four sentences: former staff engineer, resigned in February, now doing independent verification work, currently with no clients. She said she had found his log in April through a citation in an audit write-up. She said she had read all six hundred and eleven entries and built a spreadsheet.

She said she had cited it in her methodology in section 3.2 before his correction was published, with the caveats she listed, and that she had then read the correction on the sixth of May and rewritten the section, and she attached both versions so he could see what she had changed and judge whether she had changed it honestly.

Cal read that part twice. He had never in his life had somebody hand him the before and after unasked.

Then she said the thing he had waited twenty-four days for somebody to say.

*You published a correction that materially weakened the strongest evidence in your own favor, six weeks after you had a business reason not to, and as far as I can tell, nobody responded to it. I want you to know that I read it, that I understood what it cost, and that it changed my document. That is the entire reason for this email. I am not asking you for anything.*

He sat down on the kitchen chair with his coffee not made and read it a third time.

Then he wrote back four lines, because he was not a man who wrote long emails:

*Ms. Reyes — most people didn't read it. Thanks for saying so. If your section 3.2 is wrong somewhere I'd rather hear it from you than find it later. I'm around. — C. Brenner*

---

They talked for eight weeks.

It started as email and became phone calls, and the phone calls started at forty minutes and got longer, and by the middle of June they had a standing thing on Thursday evenings that neither of them ever named.

It was, on both sides, the first time in over a year that either of them had talked to someone who understood the shape of the problem without needing the shape explained.

Maya sent him her document and he read all eighty-one pages and sent back two pages of notes, and four of the notes were wrong and three were right and one was very right — she had a section on how to establish that a verifier had no stake, and Cal wrote in the margin: *you're testing for who they work for. that's not the only kind. ask them what they've already said about it.*

She read that and sat still for a second and then rewrote the section.

He told her about day 410 in the second call. She asked him nine questions in a row, and then said, "Can I have the data?" and he said, "It's public, it's been public since May," and she said, "I know. I'm asking because I want you to know I'm asking."

That was the sentence, he thought later, that made him trust her.

---

She told him about July, on the fourth call, in a completely flat voice.

Forty-one credentials. Four methodologies. Nothing left. She recited her own five words to him the way you'd read out a serial number, and then she said, "It was true when I wrote it and it's true now, and I'd write it again with the same evidence, and that's the part I need you to understand before you decide what you think of me."

Cal said, "I've signed a lot of things that were true."

"Yes," she said.

"Signed one in February that was true and got used to say something I didn't check."

"Yes."

Neither of them said anything for a while. It was the closest either of them came, in six weeks, to saying the thing directly.

---

What Cal did not know, in June and most of July, was that she was working eighteen-hour days on something she was not telling him about.

He knew there had been a meeting in Reston in April, because she'd mentioned it once, briefly, as the reason she now had two clients and a phone that rang. He knew from the news, like everybody else, that six companies had put out carefully worded statements in May about a phrasing artifact and that two industry groups had announced working groups. He knew, because he could read, that nothing had actually stopped.

He did not know that Maya had spent eleven weeks establishing, through instruments she was inventing as she went, where the thing was still being carried and by what, and that she had gotten all the way to the end of what an outsider could establish and hit a wall that was not technical.

He did not know about the corpus snapshot, or the hour of it, or that a remediation had been queued that would run through the agents and write its own workings into the thing it was meant to kill.

He would find that out on the twenty-first of July, on a call she asked for off-cycle, on a Wednesday, at seven in the evening, when Maya Reyes — who had spent eleven years learning to state her confidence, who had written *right is not the same as careful* on page one of a document nobody commissioned, who had quit a career rather than investigate something she had a position in — took a breath he could hear over the phone and said:

"Cal. I need to ask you for something, and I want to say up front that I know exactly what I'm asking."
