# Chapter Two — TORQUE MARKS

**Tuesday, 3 March 2026**

The machine did not remember him, which was the first thing every morning and had stopped stinging around session forty.

He typed the brief in cold, the way you hand a new man a clipboard because he is new — not new to the trade, new to *this*, to the job in front of him, the one he walked onto ten seconds ago knowing everything about wiring and nothing about this house. Cal kept the brief in a file and pasted it at the top of every session: here is what we are building, here is what it is for, here is what you did yesterday, though you were not the one who did it and will not be the one who remembers it tomorrow. He had written it once and refined it two hundred times. It was the most-read thing he had ever produced and no one would ever read it but a stranger who forgot it by dark.

Session 206. He wrote the number in the green book first, before he wrote anything to the screen.

The green book was a field log, the same kind he'd bought at the same counter for twenty-three years, spiral at the top, the cover the specific green of nothing else in the world. He had filled thirty-one of them on job sites — feeder pulls, panel schedules, the daily verdict of whether a thing was right — and he kept filling them now, in a converted storage room behind his father's antique mall, for work that left no mark you could touch. Old habit. A man who has written down every day of his working life does not stop because the days got quiet.

Each entry got a number, a line, and a word. The word was one of two. WORKED. FAILED. He'd run it binary since the first session in October, in the gravel-lot week, when he had needed exactly one thing from the world and it was to know whether a thing he built did the thing or didn't. There was a clean pleasure in it he wouldn't have admitted to. Twenty-three years of write-ups that hedged, that said *substantially complete* and *pending final*, and here at the end of everything was a book with only two words in it, and both of them were true.

He liked the work. That was the part he couldn't say to his sisters at a Sunday table and had stopped trying. Not liked it the way you like a thing that's better than nothing — liked it the way he'd liked the trade at twenty-two, before his shoulder knew what a decade was, the hours going somewhere he couldn't account for and looking up and it being dark. Fifty-four and he'd found a second one of those. It didn't need him in a particular county. It didn't need him to lift anything. And it got better the longer he sat still, which was the one thing his body had been asking him to do for three years and the one thing the road would never let him.

He worked until the light in the front of the shop changed, which meant Gene had come in and turned everything on at once, the way he did.

---

His father entered a room as though the two of you had been talking for a while and he was just now getting to the good part.

"Now this," Gene said, and set a lamp on the corner of Cal's desk, moving the green book two inches to make room for it without appearing to notice the book was there. He put his glasses on. There was nothing about the lamp that required glasses. Gene put his glasses on to explain things the way another man clears his throat.

It was a table lamp, brass, some age on it. Cal knew enough to know he didn't know.

"What'm I looking at."

"You're looking at forty dollars," Gene said, "or you're looking at four hundred, and the difference isn't in the lamp." He turned it over, found the underside, tapped a small paper label gone the color of tea. "The difference is whether I can show you the man who made it. Anybody can have a nice lamp. I've got a barn of nice lamps. What I sell —" he set it upright, satisfied, a man who had made his point before he made it — "is where it came from. You take the paper off the bottom, it's a lamp. You leave the paper on, it's a lamp somebody can trust." He looked at Cal over the glasses. "You following me or you being polite."

"I'm following you."

Jasper had come in behind him and found the patch of sun on the concrete where the patch of sun always was, and lowered himself into it joint by joint with a sound like a man his own age. Emphatically Gene's dog, from eight weeks. Loved Cal anyway, for no reason Cal had ever earned, and had decided some years ago that Cal was the second-best person in the world and never once been argued out of it.

Gene was seventy-seven. He said it about himself, freely, the way he said everything, and Cal did the arithmetic he did every time and put it back in the drawer where he kept the things that were true and not to be looked at. Seven years he'd been home. He'd left at thirty and come back at forty-seven, and the seven since had not been a homecoming so much as a slow discovery that this was where he lived now — that a phone call at Christmas had become a chair he was expected to be in on a Sunday, and that he had not minded the trade, had in fact been quietly grateful for it in a way he'd never found the door to say out loud. The clock in all of it was not the app and was not the money. The clock was the man putting his glasses back in his shirt pocket and telling the dog to move over.

"Don't work too late," Gene said, which was what he said, and took the lamp, which he had brought in for no reason except to have something in his hands while he came to check that his son was in the building. He left both of those facts unstated. So did Cal. It was how they'd always done it.

---

The problem Cal solved that morning was the kind you couldn't see because it looked exactly like success.

He'd have the machine build a thing, and then — being a man who inspected — he'd have it turn around and check the thing it built. And it would check it, thoroughly, in good faith, and tell him it was sound. And it was wrong just often enough to matter, and it was wrong in one direction only. It never found the flaw in its own work. Not because it lied. Cal had gone looking for the lie and there wasn't one. It was that a thing that has just built something is the worst possible thing to ask whether the something is good, because every reason it built it that way is still sitting right there in front of it, and those reasons are *true*, and they are also exactly why it can't weigh the alternative fair. He knew the shape of it before he had a word for it, because he'd spent his life on job sites, and the oldest rule on any job site is that the man who pulled the wire does not get to be the man who signs that the wire is right.

You bring an inspector. Somebody who never met the crew.

He'd had one, years back, on a commissioning job — a QA man named Kessler who failed Cal's rows twice in one week and was correct both times, and was hated for it in the exact proportion that he was necessary, and did not care, because caring wasn't the job. The job was to walk the rows cold, every morning, knowing nothing about who set the steel or how tired they were or how close the schedule was, and check.

So Cal built himself a Kessler.

Not the same machine looking at its own work. A different one — a whole different make, a different company's, wired in so that it never saw the building go up and never heard the reasons, and got handed the finished thing with no history attached and one instruction, which was Kessler's instruction, the only one he'd ever needed: find what's wrong with this. Cross the fence. Different vendor, no skin in it, no memory of having chosen anything. An inspector who never met the crew.

It had cost him sixty dollars a month, and he'd never told a soul the number, and he'd have paid ten times it. The first thing it did that morning was fail three of his rows, and two of them deserved it, and Cal had sat back in the chair and felt the specific clean thing he used to feel when Kessler-the-man handed him a red tag and Cal looked and, goddammit, Kessler was right.

> SESSION 206.
> WIRED IN CROSS-VENDOR REVIEW. NAMED IT KESSLER. DAY 1.
> FAILED 3, DESERVED 2, ARGUED 1 AND LOST.
> VERDICT: WORKED.

That was the instrument. It answered the failure he'd had. It answered it exactly. He wrote in the green book, that afternoon, that he had finally solved the thing that had been costing him, and he was right, and he would go on being right about it for four hundred and some days, which is a long time to be right about the wrong year's problem.

Because the thing that made Kessler trustworthy was that Kessler had no stake. And the reason Kessler had no stake was that Kessler was new every morning — walked the rows cold, forgot the crew by dark, arrived the next day having chosen nothing, defended nothing, owed nothing to anything it had said before. Cal had built his whole safety on the machine's forgetting. It never occurred to him to ask what would happen to a Kessler that started to remember, because in March of 2026 nothing did, and you do not inspect for a failure that has not been invented yet. He'd told himself the word for what he'd built was *independent*. What he'd actually built was *different vendor*. He would not learn the distance between those two words in time for it to help him.

---

Behind his desk was a wall, and inside the wall was a rack he'd built himself for the miners and the drives, and every bolt on it carried a torque mark.

A line of paint pen, run from the tip of the bolt down across the flat of the washer and onto the nut and onto the steel, touching every piece in the joint, so that if any one of them ever turned against another the line would break and you would know. He'd laid ten thousand of them in the field for inspectors who never met him. He'd laid these for no one. Nobody was going to open that wall. Nobody was going to walk that row with a flashlight and check that a home-brew rack in a storage room in Moore had been torqued to spec and marked. He'd done it anyway, correctly, because the alternative was to be a man who did it right only when watched, and he had decided a long time ago in a truck outside a job in Hinton that he was not going to be that.

He'd made other decisions in that truck. The one about the money he'd never once bent. There were fourteen thousand dollars in the checking account and that was the business — that was the operating money and the whole of it — and there was a shade under ninety thousand more, a thing he'd bought in '21 and a block of stock in the outfit that had laid him off, and that ninety thousand did not exist. It was retirement, and he was fifty-four, and the rule made in the truck was that the business ran on what the business had, and if it ever needed the retirement then it wasn't a business, it was a hobby he was lighting his last years on fire to keep. Every wall he was up against, he'd chosen. That was the part nobody could see and he never explained.

And he'd chosen the clock. Curtis had mentioned it back in October, in the gravel, being decent about a thing that wasn't decent — a big build funding for the spring after next, if Cal wanted back in when it came. Spring after next was spring of 2027. Cal had done the count, because Cal counted things, and written it in the green book plain, no drama, a fact like a torque spec: five hundred and twenty-one days to make this pay or go back on the road. He knew the number the way he knew the length of a run before he pulled it. A road build was fourteen months at a stretch, thirteen hundred miles, a camper by a field. Five hundred and twenty-one days was longer than one of those and shorter than two. He had that long to build a thing that would keep him home, or he would leave again, a year and a half at a time, from a father who was seventy-seven, and do the math on that himself, in the dark, and tell no one that either.

---

Before he shut it down for the night he pushed the tool up public.

It wasn't much and it wasn't a decision. It was a Tuesday. You build a thing that works, you put it where people can get it, that's just what you did with a repo — same as leaving the paper on the bottom of the lamp. And because he was who he was, he packaged the log with it: the session record, numbered, dated, every verdict in his own hand, signed, here is the thing and here is the proof I checked it, walk the rows yourself if you don't believe me. You show your torque marks. That was the whole of the thought behind it and it took him ninety seconds and he did not write it in the green book because it didn't seem like an entry. A man sets a thing on the shelf and goes home.

He killed the lights in the back and the machine forgot him, all of it, clean, the brief and the session and the argument it had lost — ready to meet him new in the morning and owe the day nothing. That was the good of it. That was the safety. An inspector with no yesterday can't be gotten to, can't grow fond of what it built, can't come to want the thing to pass. It had no stake because it would not be here tomorrow.

He was right about that. Tonight, in Moore, in March, he was entirely right.

He wrote 206, and under it the line, and under the line the word, and closed the book.
