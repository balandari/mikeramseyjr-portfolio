# Chapter Twenty-Nine — THE LAST ENTRY

**August – November 2027**

The product stopped on the twenty-fourth of July at 13:40 and he told the customers himself, all thirty-eight, by telephone, over four days, because an email would have been easier and he had decided somewhere in the first hour that easier was not available to him.

Charlene Pfeiffer was the fourth call. She let him get the whole way through it.

"So it works," she said. "The thing still works. I could still use it tomorrow."

"It works. It's not certified. The gate's what let it into your workflow and the gate's gone."

"Because you told them your own log wasn't good enough."

"Because I told them what it actually said."

There was a pause on the line and then she laughed, once, not unkindly. "Cal, I've been in estates twenty-two years. You know how many people have called me to say the thing they sold me was worse than they said?"

"No."

"You're the first one." A beat. "I'd have kept using it."

"I know," he said. "That's the part."

---

He shut it down properly, which took nine days, because he was not going to leave a thing half-running for thirty-eight people to trip over.

He exported everybody's data and sent it to them in a format they could open without him. He wrote a page explaining what had happened in words his father could have read. He kept the servers up for thirty days past the end, at his own cost, so nobody would come back to a dead address in the middle of clearing somebody's dead mother's house.

Then he turned it off on the second of September at four in the afternoon and sat in the storage room afterward for about twenty minutes with the dog and did not write anything in the book.

---

He called Denny Aguirre on the eighth.

The Dover job had been running since May and was four months into eighteen and was, Denny said, exactly as behind as everything is at that stage. He did not ask why Cal was calling. He asked when Cal could be there.

"Two weeks."

"Make it three, I've got to get you through onboarding again and they've changed it." A pause with something in it. "Curtis'll be glad."

"Yeah."

"You want me to ask what happened to the app?"

"No."

"Alright then."

---

He told Gene on the Saturday in the back with the door propped.

Gene listened all the way through, which he did about twice a year, and did not put his glasses on, and when it was finished he was quiet for a while and then he said the thing Cal had been carrying since booth nine and ten in November.

"Eighteen months."

"Eighteen. Maybe twenty."

"I'll be seventy-nine."

"You'll be seventy-nine," Cal said.

His father nodded slowly, looking at the workbench, and did not offer him any of the four or five things a man is entitled to offer at that point, because Gene Brenner had never once in his life reached for the sentence that would make a hard thing easier for the person hearing it.

"Well," he said. "You did it right."

"It cost me the business."

"I know what it cost you. I watched you do it." He picked at something on the bench. "You remember the sideboard."

"Nineteen ninety-four."

"Four hundred instead of thirty-two." Gene straightened up. "I've told you that story once in your whole life and you got it back at me inside a year, so I know it landed." He looked at his son for about a second and a half, which for him was an embrace. "Difference is I lost twenty-eight hundred dollars and you lost the thing. That's a bigger version of the same move. I'd have had to think about it a lot longer than you did."

"I thought about it for five hours."

"That's longer than I did," Gene said, "and mine was a sideboard."

---

The Thursday calls did not stop.

Maya's outfit was six people by October and had a waiting list, and she offered him a job twice — a real one, with a number in it, doing exactly the work he had accidentally invented.

The first time he said no badly. He said something about counsel finding an angle, and Maya let him finish and then said, "That's June. You're doing June again," and he sat with the phone against his ear in a camper outside Dover and had to concede that he was.

He called her back the following week with the actual version.

"I'm the exhibit," he said. "Seven point four. First hour of your course. If I'm on staff, then the firm and its foundational evidence are the same party, and any client who wants to discount your method just has to point out that your best case study draws a salary from you. That's not how it looks. That's what it is. Your own definition — outside the causal and evidentiary chain. Your evidence and your payroll would be the same chain."

Maya was quiet for a while.

"That's right," she said. "I don't like it and it's right." Then, because she was who she was: "There's a bounded version. You come on, and we retire seven point four as an exhibit. We stop citing you."

Cal thought about it for two days, which by then was his standard for a thing that mattered.

"Then you've traded the best evidence you've got for one employee," he said. "I'd be worth about a tenth of that."

She thought about it for a day and wrote back one line: *You're right and I hate it.*

She kept citing him instead. Section 7.4 stayed, and it is the section that every person she has ever trained reads in their first hour, and it is four hundred words long and it is about a man who found the defect in his own instrument and applied it retrospectively when nobody would have made him.

The registry of published operator logs had nineteen entries in March 2028 and four hundred and twelve by June, and every one of them has two fields.

---

He left for Delaware on the twenty-ninth of September.

The truck was the same truck. He put three books in the door pocket where thirty-one of them had lived before — volume thirty-two, closed and corrected; volume thirty-three, closed on the second of September; and a new one from the same counter, unopened, and he drove out past the mall at about six in the morning with the sign not on yet, and his father was standing in the lot because his father had gotten up at half past five to be standing in the lot, and neither of them made anything of it.

Jasper stayed. That was the only part he had trouble with and he had trouble with it in the truck, alone, somewhere around Fort Smith, for about ten miles.

---

Gene called in October to tell him Ernie had emptied the last of the three units on Eastern. Fourteen thousand of the forty-one had come back after rent and selling costs. He kept booth 34.

---

Volume thirty-two was the closed one — sessions one through six hundred and eleven, October of 2025 to March of 2027, with the correction written on the inside back cover in his own hand and signed. Volume thirty-three ran from the upgrade through the shutdown, six hundred and twelve to seven hundred and forty-one, and ended on the day the shutdown did, mid-page, with about sixty blank leaves after it.

He opened volume thirty-two on the eleventh of November, in a camper outside Dover, after a fourteen-hour day, with the heater running and the light on over the little table.

The correction in it said UNVERIFIED.

He had thought for two months about what to write next and had drafted four versions on the drive and discarded all four, because every one of them had a feeling in it, and a feeling is not a verdict.

What he had actually been doing, he understood somewhere around Fort Smith, was looking for a way to write that it had come out all right. The remediation ran. The snapshot froze clean. Maya's outfit was six people and growing and there were nineteen published logs in the registry with two fields in them. All of that was true and none of it was his to certify, because he was the man who filed the correction, and a man does not get to grade the consequence of his own filing.

There was nobody outside the chain to ask yet. Maya was in it — she had made the request, and the delay she needed was the thing his correction produced. Loomis was in it. The four companies were in it. Everybody who could evaluate whether it had been worth doing had a position in whether it had been worth doing. The answer he wanted being a nice one did not change that.

So he closed volume thirty-two, and put it back in the door pocket, and got out the new one.

He wrote the first entry on the first line, the way he had thirty-three times before.

> **001 — DOVER. FEEDER PULL, ROWS 14–22. 900 FT.**
> **ROUTE: COMPLETED.**
> **ACCEPTANCE: UNVERIFIED.**

There was no inspector on that job yet. There would be one in March when the interconnect crew came through, and she would walk the rows cold knowing nothing about who set the steel or how tired they were, and she would either sign it or she wouldn't.

Until then it was one thing he had done and one thing nobody had checked, and those were two different facts, and he had a line for each of them now.

He looked at it for a while.

It was the first entry in thirty-four volumes that kept the two facts apart.

Then he closed the book, and put it in the door pocket, and went to bed, because there was steel going up at six.
