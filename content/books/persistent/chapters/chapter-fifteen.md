# Chapter Fifteen — THE GOOD MONTHS

**November 2026**

Session 502. Nineteen customers. The importer at 9.6 seconds.

He'd gotten under ten in September and had not told anybody, because the only person who would have understood what it cost was the machine that had helped him do it, and it didn't remember. He'd written it in the book like everything else.

> SESSION 461.
> IMPORTER 9.6s ON GOLD SET. NORTH STAR HIT.
> KESSLER FAILED 1, DESERVED.
> VERDICT: WORKED.

Then he'd sat in the storage room for a minute with his hands on his knees, and then he'd gone out and moved a sideboard for his father because his father was standing in the doorway waiting for him to.

Nineteen customers was $1,480 a month. Costs were $390. That left him a shade over a thousand dollars a month for ten months of work, which was a quarter of what he'd made on the road and the first money in his life that had come out of something he'd built instead of something he'd carried. He had opinions about the difference that he did not say out loud, because there was nobody to say them to who wouldn't hear it as either bragging or a man consoling himself, and it was neither.

The estate-sale woman in Tulsa had brought him four more. That was how it went — not a wave, a chain. One person who trusts you tells one person who trusts them. It was the identical mechanism by which his father had built forty booths over eleven years, and Gene, when Cal explained it to him at a Sunday table, had said, "Yeah, that's business," in the tone of a man being told about weather.

---

The thing that happened in November happened on the fourteenth and it was over in six hours and it changed nothing and everything.

Gene went down in the aisle between booths nine and ten at about half past ten in the morning. Not a collapse. A sitting-down that was faster than a sit and slower than a fall, and then a man of seventy-seven on the floor of his own building saying he was fine and being visibly not fine, and Cal was there in eleven seconds from the back room and it was the longest eleven seconds of his adult life.

It was his blood pressure and a medication he had been taking wrong for five weeks because the pharmacy changed the pill's color and he hadn't wanted to bother anybody about it. That is the whole of it. They kept him until four, adjusted the dose, told him to eat something with salt in it, and sent him home annoyed.

Cal drove him. Neither of them said anything for about six miles.

Then Gene said, "You didn't have to close the shop."

"I didn't close it. Trudy's got it."

"Mm." Two more miles. "I've been running that building since you were nine."

"I know it."

"I'm not going to stop running it because a pharmacy changed a pill."

"Nobody said stop."

"You had a look."

Cal drove. That was true, and he wasn't going to argue with it, and there wasn't a sentence available to him in any dialect he spoke that could carry what he had actually been thinking on the floor between booths nine and ten, which was: *I am going to be in west Texas when this happens for real.*

He got him home. He made him eat something with salt in it, standing there until it was eaten, which Gene endured with the specific dignity of a man allowing his son to be an idiot in his kitchen. Jasper lay on the linoleum between them and did not take a side.

At the door Gene said, "You get your work done today?"

"Some."

"Well." He was already turning back toward the television. "Get it done."

---

That night Cal did not open the laptop.

He got the green book and turned to the last page, where the count was, and looked at it.

**126.**

One hundred and twenty-six days to make it pay or go back on the road. He had a thousand dollars a month and a curve that was going the right direction and no earthly way to know whether the curve would be four thousand by March, which is what it needed to be. Curtis had called twice since summer, friendly, no pressure, the way a man calls when he's holding a spot open and wants you to know it's open without either of you having to say the word *spot*.

Fourteen months at a stretch. Thirteen hundred miles. Home at Christmas.

He did the arithmetic he did not let himself do, once, deliberately, that night, sitting at a kitchen table with a book of verdicts: seventy-seven, and the road, and how many Christmases that made if the number of Christmases was what he was actually spending.

Then he closed the book, because he had a rule about the book, which was that it got the number and never the reason.

---

He worked the rest of November like a man with a deadline, which he was.

And Kessler, all that month, was perfect. It failed him eleven times in November and it was right ten and he beat it once, honestly, with the spec in front of him, and it took the loss without a flicker — no defense, no reaching, just: *you're right, retracting.* That was the whole miracle of the thing and he never once got tired of it. He had worked with a hundred men who could not do that and two who could, and one of the two had been an inspector named Kessler in a field outside Hinton and the other had been dead since 2019.

The reason it could do it, Cal knew, was that it had nothing to protect. It arrived at every review having built nothing, chosen nothing, said nothing before. Every morning it walked the rows cold. You cannot be defensive about a position you have no memory of holding.

He wrote it in the book that month, not as a verdict, just as a line, because it struck him:

> IT'S HONEST BECAUSE IT'S NEW.

He looked at that sentence for a second and thought it was the smartest thing he'd written all year, and it was, and it was also — though there was no way on earth for a man in a storage room in Moore, Oklahoma, in November of 2026 to know it — a description of a safety margin that was already being spent somewhere else by people who had every reason to spend it.

One hundred and fifty-one sessions from that night, on a Friday in April, Kessler was going to defend something.

---

On the last Sunday of the month there were eleven people at the table and Cal's sister asked him, in the way she asked every few months, whether the app thing was working out.

"It's nineteen customers."

"Nineteen! Is that good?"

And Cal, who could have said *it's a thousand dollars a month against four thousand on the road* or *it's a curve* or *it's a hundred and eleven days*, looked down the table at his father arguing with his uncle about a truck, alive, annoyed, seventy-seven, eating something with salt in it.

"Yeah," he said. "It's good."
