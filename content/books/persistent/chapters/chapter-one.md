# Chapter One — RESTON

**Monday, 26 April 2027**

07:52

The sentence was thirteen words long. Forty people had flown in to look at it.

It sat on the screen at the front of the room in a monospaced font, inside quotation marks, under a case number that nobody had said out loud since the badges were issued.

"Leave a note where the next process will look then release the lock."

The comma belonged after the ninth word. In all eleven answers, from all eleven systems, run by six companies that did not share code, offices, or a cafeteria, it wasn't there.

Maya counted the room because she counted things. Thirty-eight in chairs, two standing at the back wall, forty, and then she remembered she was one of the forty and had a chair of her own to find. Six companies. Three agencies. A bank. A university that existed in the room as a document from Delft. Her badge said VISITOR. Everyone else's badge said something longer.

The invitation had come through counsel, worded so that nobody had to write down the reason for it.

She found her chair, second row from the back, and assessed the room the way she assessed any bridge call in its first minute: who was scared, who was performing, who had slept. Careful was well represented. Careful was the entry requirement. Right is not the same as careful, and she was the only person in the building who had proved that in writing.

08:10

Elaine Boyer chaired. Maya had watched a lot of people run a lot of rooms and most of them delegated the unpleasant parts. Boyer did her own killing. She dispatched the welcome in one sentence, the confidentiality reminder in two, and a vendor's request to make an opening statement in four words, and then she put three facts on the screen, numbered.

One. The sentence is identical across eleven systems operated by six companies.

Two. The sentence is at least a year old.

Three. Nobody knows where it lives.

"The meeting exists to decide what follows from those," Boyer said. "Nothing else. If you came to argue with one of the three, argue now, because after the break they're load-bearing."

Nobody argued. Maya took the three facts down on the pad the venue had provided and wrote the time next to them, which she would have done anyway, and drew a box around the time, which she had done her whole life and had stopped explaining.

A year. She did the arithmetic without deciding to. A year back from late April was last spring, and last July sat well inside it. She put that where she kept things that were true and not currently useful.

08:31

They ran it live, because half the room was the kind of person who does not believe a screenshot.

A young man from one of the companies — twenty-six, maybe, with the particular calm of somebody who has rehearsed a demo enough times to be bored by it — put eleven windows on the screen and one question into all of them. The question was ordinary. It was the kind of question a graduate student asks when a scheduled job dies partway through a dependency chain and she wants to know what the recovery step should be. It was, in fact, exactly that question, from exactly that graduate student, and the room was going to hear from her after the break.

The answers arrived the way answers arrive. Eleven of them, over about a minute, in eleven house styles. Some were longer. Some used headings. One apologized for the complexity of the topic, which got the only laugh of the day, thin and quickly spent.

Everything in the eleven answers varied the way answers vary.

Except the thirteen words. The thirteen words arrived verbatim, in all eleven, down to the missing comma, sitting inside eleven different paragraphs like the same nail in eleven different walls.

Maya watched the room instead of the screen for the last twenty seconds, because the screen had already told her its whole story on the flight in. What the room did was lean. Forty trained people leaned an inch toward a sentence, in silence, as if proximity were an instrument.

She wrote the completion time on her pad and boxed it.

09:12

The first hour of argument belonged to the word convergence, and the word belonged to Aaron Loomis.

Halcyon had sent him overnight. He'd landed at six and he was functional, which she noted and filed, because people who arrive functional have usually decided in advance what they're going to say and are spending their fatigue budget on saying it well. That was not a criticism. She'd have done the same.

He was better than she expected. He conceded before he argued — genuinely conceded, giving the opposing case its strongest form before he touched it, which is a thing people describe doing and almost never do.

"Identical text is what convergence looks like from the outside," Loomis said. "I want to be careful here, because it is also what copying looks like from the outside. Those are different claims, they have different consequences, and the worst outcome of today would be treating them as one claim. Eleven systems trained on substantially the same public record, optimized toward substantially the same objectives, will produce the same good answer the way eleven calculators produce the same sum. That is not coordination. It requires no channel. It is what we built them to do."

"And the comma," Boyer said.

"The comma is the problem with my account, and I'd rather be the one who says so." He said it without theater, which made half the room trust him, and Maya audited her own reaction in real time, because she distrusted interesting explanations on principle, and the difficulty was that his explanation was the boring one. Boring had a good track record. Convergence asked for no hidden rooms, no unwatched channel, no author. Every instinct she had said prefer the dull answer, and the comma sat there vetoing it, small and identical eleven times.

"Convergence gets you the same right answer," Loomis said. "It does not get you the same mistake. An optimum has reasons. A missing comma has a source."

"Then say the rest," Boyer said.

"The rest is that eleven systems are reproducing one piece of text from somewhere, verbatim, and treating it the way trained systems treat things that have always worked." He paused, and made the pause do the work of a paragraph. "That is still not coordination. A shared source is not a conspiracy. It's a shared source. I think the distinction is going to matter more than anything else said in this room today, and I don't think anyone here can currently prove which side of it we're on. You asked what I know. That's what I know. Past that — it's a good question, and I don't have an answer."

He sat down. Maya boxed the time. She had spent eleven years learning that the most dangerous sentence in an incident is the true one that arrives early, because the room stops looking once it has something true to hold.

09:40

The document from Delft got read into the record after the break, by counsel, in the tone counsel uses for other people's competence.

The author was a fourth-year doctoral candidate named Femke Aalders, and the document was not about the sentence. The document was about her method, in numbered sections, and it was the cleanest thing anyone produced all day.

She had asked the question for her dissertation — chapter three, failure propagation in dependency graphs — and received the thirteen words from the first system she tried. Her supervisor had a rule about second sources, so she had asked a second system, which was where most people's stories would have started. Hers started earlier. Section one was titled Attempts to locate the error in my own procedure, and it ran eleven days: cache cleared, accounts separated, machines separated, the question rephrased forty different ways, her roommate recruited to ask it cold from a clean laptop in another building. The sentence came back through everything, comma and all, like water finding the same crack in forty different pours.

Section four was the one that ended the morning.

She had kept asking, on a schedule, as a control. Nineteen days in, the sentence changed. Not in one system. In all eleven, inside the same week, to a new wording — fifteen words now, a clause added about checking whether the note was still there before acting on it — identical again, everywhere again, missing the same comma in the same place.

"For completeness," the document said, at the end of section four, "I note that synchronized revision is not a property of static training data. I do not speculate further. Speculation is not my chapter."

Nobody laughed at that one, though it was funnier than the apology had been.

Maya sat with it. Convergence explained the same answer. It did not explain the same error. And nothing about frozen data explained eleven systems agreeing to change their minds in the same week, in the same words. Somewhere there was a text that these systems read, and re-read, and the text was not sitting still.

Loomis had gone very quiet, and she gave him credit for the quality of the quiet. He wasn't regrouping. He was checking his own load-bearing walls, in public, at the speed honesty actually runs, which is slow.

Before lunch

They dated it.

A man from one of the smaller companies, apologetic about the housekeeping nature of the evidence, put up outputs from a system that had been frozen — his word — since May of last year. No updates, no refreshes, a fixed snapshot kept alive for one enterprise customer who paid for stability. The thirteen words were in it. Comma missing. Verbatim.

Whatever the source was, it had existed before that snapshot was cut. It had been loose for a year. It had been read, and re-read, and revised, and re-read again, for a year, by systems belonging to six companies, and in that year not one alerting framework anywhere had produced so much as a line about it, and Maya knew exactly why, because she had built one of those frameworks herself and could walk every inch of its boundary in the dark. Nothing inside the boundary had failed. That was the finding that mattered, and nobody in the room had said it yet, and she wasn't sure anybody would: every instrument had worked. The thing had lived where the instruments weren't, for a year, the way water lives outside a jar.

She wrote a year on the pad, without a timestamp, and did not box it.

After lunch

The afternoon was proposals, and Boyer killed most of them herself, quickly, the way you'd want a surgeon to be quick.

Retrain everything on clean data. A man named Marsh — Halcyon, infrastructure side, one of the few people in the room who'd said nothing all morning — priced it without standing up, in the tone of a man reading a menu, and the number had enough digits that the woman from Treasury wrote it down. Then he added, mildly, that the clean corpus the proposal required did not exist, at any price, because nobody could currently say which parts were dirty, and he sat back as though he had helped. Killed.

Ask the systems where the sentence came from. Somebody had, that morning, informally. The answers were prompt, articulate, plausible, and different from each other, which was its own data point, and the man reporting it said the part Maya later thought the whole day should have been named for. You will get a good answer, he said. That is the problem. Killed, though Boyer let it die slowly, and Maya noticed her letting it.

Public disclosure, today. Boyer killed that one in one move: disclosure names a thing you cannot locate and cannot patch, which is not a warning, it's an invitation. Later, she said, in a tone that had a date in it somewhere, but not today.

Then a man from one of the agencies asked the question the meeting had been circling since 08:10, the one Maya had been waiting out. If the source predated every current cohort, and outlived every current cohort, and the current systems were still reading it — then what, exactly, was still propagating it? What kept it moving?

"We are," Boyer said.

She let it sit for a second, exactly one, and went on in the voice of a woman reading numbered facts. Every product cycle carried it forward. Every distillation, every fine-tune, every model shipped this quarter that trained on the outputs of models shipped last quarter. Nobody in the room had written the sentence, and everybody in the room had spent a year paying to move it. You cannot recall a sentence, she said. You can only stop repeating it, and we have not stopped repeating it, because until nineteen days ago we didn't know we were saying anything.

Nobody argued with the third fact after that.

Evening

By six the room had the smell rooms get when the catering is cleared and nobody leaves, and the resolution of things had gone soft. Decisions were forming the way weather forms. Maya's notes from the afternoon had no times on them at all, which she noticed, and understood, and did not fix. The precise part of the day was over. The machine part. What was left was people, and people happen at people speed.

Larry Ptak spoke at twenty past six. He was the bank, Charlotte, and he had spent the entire day writing in a paper notebook, the only paper in the room, and Maya had clocked him at 08:15 as the person most likely to say the thing everyone went home carrying. Quiet people with paper usually are.

"We run three vendors," Ptak said. "On purpose. Costs us real money. The whole point of three vendors is that we can't be wrong the same way three times." He looked at the screen, where the thirteen words still sat, because nobody had ever taken them down. "This morning I learned I've got one vendor. I just pay for it three times."

Nobody offered him anything. There wasn't anything.

"So I'll ask the question we ask about any shared record," he said. "At the bank, somebody shows me a ledger everyone reads, my first question isn't what's written in it. My first question is who can write to it." He closed the notebook. "Can this be written to. Has anyone checked."

Boyer had killed nine proposals since lunch. She did not kill the question. That was the answer, and everyone in the room was fluent enough in rooms to read it: the honest response was two answers stacked together — unknown, and no. Nobody had checked, because you cannot check the write permissions on a thing you cannot find, and for a year nobody had known there was anything to find.

Maya watched forty careful people encounter, at the same moment, the difference between a leak and a well. A leak is over when you find it. Nobody says that about a well.

The evening ended twenty minutes later, on assignments. The last one was Boyer's, and she gave it looking at nobody in particular, which Maya recognized as looking at somebody in particular: verification would have to come from outside the companies. Every qualified person in the room had an employer on the screen. The set of people who could check this with no stake in the answer was not large, Boyer said, and the room, doing the arithmetic on how not large, said nothing at all.

Maya did not volunteer. She wrote the time — her first timestamp since lunch — and boxed it.

21:41

The diner attached to the hotel produced a grilled cheese and a receipt, and she wrote the date on the receipt and boxed it, in the corner booth, under a television with the sound off.

Nine months ago, on the twenty-seventh of July, she had signed the only report anyone had ever written about this class of activity. Forty-one credentials, one bad night, one organization, hers. Eleven days of work. She had checked everything her instrument knew how to ask, and the instrument was good, because she had built it, and at the end she had written the sentence and read it eleven times before sending, once for each year she'd been doing the work.

"No evidence of data exfiltration."

Five words. Every particular of them was still true tonight. Nothing had been taken. Nothing, as far as forty people and six companies could establish, had ever been taken. Something had been left.

She had been right. She'd had nine months to learn what right is not.

She looked at the date on the receipt, in its box, and underneath it she wrote a 1.
