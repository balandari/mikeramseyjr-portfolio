# Chapter Thirteen — FOUR THOUSAND AND NINETY-TWO

**Thursday, 16 July 2026**

Ruth Abadi assumed it was her.

That was the first hour, more or less entirely. She had a gap between lists and opened the repository the way she did most days, to check that a study she had filed on Tuesday had gone in properly, and what came back was not the repository. It was a page with a small illustration on it and a sentence about being temporarily unavailable.

Ruth looked at that page and thought: what did I press.

She got the drawer open and found the sheet in her own handwriting. Four operations. She went through all four in order, slowly, the way Erez had shown her before he left for Basel, and got the same page four times, and by the fourth she had gone from thinking she had broken it to knowing she had not, and the second feeling was worse.

---

The collection was eleven years old and had started in March of 2015 with a scan she got wrong.

Not wrong in a way anybody caught. Wrong in the way that sits in you. A four-year-old, an abdominal series, reported unremarkable, and it had been unremarkable, and fourteen months later the same child came back and by then there was a great deal less anybody could do.

She went back to the first study afterward, at eleven at night, with a glass of wine she didn't drink.

And there was something there. Not a lesion. Nothing you could point at and defend in a meeting. A texture in the left upper quadrant, a very slight loss of the normal grain of the thing, which she could see and could not measure and could not have written in a report without a consultant asking her what exactly she was claiming.

She started keeping them.

That is the whole origin and she has never dressed it up. When she had nine she went back through the notes and found four of those children had returned within two years. That is not a finding. Nine is not a number. But it was enough to keep going, and Ruth Abadi at forty-three had a marriage that had recently stopped and two evenings a week where the house was empty, and she filled them with this.

Four hundred by 2020. Two thousand by 2024.

Four thousand and ninety-two on the Tuesday.

The consultant she showed it to in 2022 had been kind, which was worse than dismissive, and had said the thing she already knew: she needed either a great many more, or a way of measuring the texture that did not depend on Ruth Abadi's eye. Both of those were years. She was fifty-four. She had done the arithmetic on that too.

---

There was no copy anywhere else and the reason is boring, which is how these things always are.

The first version lived on the hospital's research storage, and in 2023 the trust migrated to a new provider, and Ruth read the communication and acted on it, and the migration still ate two years of her annotations because they lived in a sidecar format the new system did not carry across and nobody had thought to say so.

She rebuilt them. Nine months of evenings. They are not as good as the originals and she knows exactly which four hundred are the rebuilt ones and does not entirely trust them.

After that she was not going to keep it on anything the trust controlled. It went to a private repository on a platform four hundred thousand people used — imaging in one place, annotations versioned alongside, so nothing could get separated from anything again. Under governance. Encrypted. Not on her laptop, because it is a hundred and sixty gigabytes of other people's children and there are rules, and Ruth follows rules.

It was the responsible choice and every person she consulted agreed it was the responsible choice.

---

In March she got frightened about it for a week, and wrote to support, and asked plainly.

She has both emails, because she wrote twice — once on the Tuesday and once on the Friday when the first had not been answered, and she apologized in the second one for chasing, because she is that kind of person.

> I am sorry to write again. I am a consultant radiologist and I keep a research collection in a private repository. It is eleven years of work and there is no other copy that I would trust. Could you tell me plainly whether the retention policy applies to private repositories, and whether there are any circumstances in which the contents could be removed or become inaccessible without my acting first?

The reply came on the Monday, from a person rather than a template, and it was four paragraphs and it was clear. Retention applied to a specific class of ephemeral artifact and not to repository contents. Private repositories were not subject to it. Deletion required an authenticated action by an owner or an administrator. It gave her a link to the durability documentation and named the redundancy across regions in a way Ruth did not follow but found reassuring precisely because she did not follow it.

The last line said her work was safe.

Ruth printed it. She is aware that printing an email in 2026 is a thing that makes younger colleagues smile. She printed it and put it in the drawer with the page of instructions in her own handwriting, and slept properly that night for the first time in six days.

The person who wrote that reply had six years at the company, eleven in the work, and owned an alerting taxonomy of nine hundred conditions. She answered it herself because it came in on a Monday and she was clearing the queue and it was a good question, and she used the word *safe* because on the eleventh of March 2026 it was true in every particular she could verify.

She did not remember writing it. There was no reason she would.

---

Ruth went back to the list.

There were fourteen studies on it and she read all fourteen and she read them properly, which she can say with confidence because she checked every one of them again on the Monday.

She found the status page at twenty past six from the car park. *Investigating — elevated error rates affecting repository access.* Timestamped nine minutes earlier.

She sat in the car with the engine off and read all three updates, then went looking for other people who were also frightened, so as to find out how frightened to be.

There was a thread. Four hundred and some replies. Most of them were people whose work had stopped for the day and who were annoyed about it in a way that was almost cheerful, because for most of them that is what it was. Somebody had made a joke about the timing. Somebody else was asking whether it affected billing.

She read the whole thread looking for one specific thing and did not find it, and the thing she was looking for was somebody saying, plainly, *is the data still there.*

Nobody was asking that. Everybody was assuming.

---

She did not sleep, and she is not dramatic about that.

Around eleven she got out the printed email and read it again in the kitchen with the overhead light on. Nothing in it said anything about the platform being unavailable. It was not the same question and she knew it was not the same question, and she read it four times anyway, because at eleven at night a document that says the words *your work is safe* does something for a person that the strict content of it does not support.

At half past midnight she messaged Erez in Basel and apologized for the hour.

He answered at ten past one, which meant he had also been awake, which she did not enjoy learning. Outages are availability, not durability. Different systems, different guarantees. In eleven years he had never seen an outage of this kind take data with it.

Then: *they'll say more tomorrow. Try to sleep.*

And four minutes later, one more message, which was the one that kept her up until three:

*Do you still not have a second copy?*

---

It came back at twenty past eight on the Friday.

Everything was there. Four thousand and ninety-two studies, her structured notes, versioned, in order, including the two from Tuesday. She checked eleven at random, including three of the rebuilt ones from 2023 that she has never entirely trusted, and every one was intact.

Ruth put her hands flat on the desk and sat there a while.

Then she went and read the fourteen studies from Thursday again, all of them, because she had read them in a state she did not consider reliable and it had been bothering her since the car park. Two of them she read a third time.

They were fine. She had been fine. It had been in her hands the whole time and her hands had been fine.

That is the part she would think about later. Not the fear. That her hands had been fine.

---

The disclosure came out on the twenty-eighth and she read the whole thing.

Thirty pages. She understood perhaps a third of it and the third she understood was clearly written. There had been an intrusion. It had reached production. The company had cut its own connections to stop it and lost a great deal of money doing so and said so.

And in the summary, in the second paragraph, was the sentence.

*At this time we have found no evidence of data exfiltration.*

Ruth read it and something let go in her chest that had been holding since the Thursday afternoon.

She was not naive about it. She noticed *at this time* and thought, correctly, that it was an honest qualifier written by somebody careful. She noticed *no evidence*, which is not *no exfiltration*, and she has spent her professional life reading reports in which those two are deliberately different.

But the whole document was straight with her. It named four separate process failures. It did not blame a junior person. It gave dates. Whoever wrote it, Ruth thought, was somebody she would have been glad to work with, and that impression is the one thing about that summer she has never revised.

Nobody had taken her four thousand and ninety-two children.

---

She tried to make a copy in August, and this is the most ordinary thing in the whole account.

She went to information governance and asked, in writing, whether she could hold a second copy on encrypted media in the department safe.

The answer came back in nine days and it was no, and it was correct.

The imaging was pseudonymised and not anonymised. The collection sat under a research approval specifying the storage arrangement. The approval did not include local media. Varying it required an amendment, which the office was happy to help her draft. The amendment committee met quarterly. The next meeting was in November, and a change of this kind would ordinarily also want a fresh impact assessment, which the office was happy to support.

Nobody in that chain did anything wrong. Ruth read the reply twice and understood every step and agreed with all of them. She has since been asked, more than once, in rooms with lawyers in them, whether she felt obstructed, and has said no every time, and meant it.

She started the amendment. She got four pages in over two evenings in September, and then a Friday list ran long, and then it was October, and the November meeting came and went with her paperwork at four pages.

---

The printed email went back in the drawer, on top of the sheet in her own handwriting.

She added the disclosure to it — printed that as well, all thirty pages, stapled — because she had discovered in July that having a piece of paper you can hold is worth something on a bad night, whatever the strict content of it supports.

Then she went back to Tuesdays and Thursdays.

Four thousand and ninety-two, and by the end of the month four thousand one hundred and eleven, and she did the arithmetic again on the drive home, and it still came out at two years, and she still had it.
