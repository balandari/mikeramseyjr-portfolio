# Chapter Twenty-Three — SECTION FOUR

**Delft, 4 March – 22 April 2027**

Femke Aalders was in the fourth year of a doctorate on failure propagation in dependency graphs and she had a supervisor with one rule, which was: never a single source.

The rule was not about machines. Bram had been saying it since 1998 and he said it about everything — papers, colleagues, his own memory. He said it the way other people say *look both ways*. Femke had heard it perhaps four hundred times and had adopted it the way you adopt a parent's grammar, without deciding to.

So on the fourth of March, when she asked a question for chapter three and got an answer, she asked it somewhere else.

The question was ordinary. She was writing about recovery from an interrupted process holding a lock with work queued behind it, and she wanted a plain-language statement of standard practice to argue against in section 3.4, because her whole chapter was an argument that standard practice was wrong in a specific bounded case.

The first answer contained a thirteen-word sentence.

The second answer contained the same thirteen-word sentence.

She noticed the comma immediately, because she was Dutch and had been taught English punctuation by a woman in Utrecht with strong feelings, and because the sentence was doing something her ear didn't like.

---

She did not tell anyone for eleven days.

This is the part that people find strange afterward and that Femke considers the least strange thing she did. She did not tell anyone because she did not yet have anything to tell. She had two identical strings and a hypothesis about her own procedure, and her procedure was, by an enormous margin, the most likely source of error in the room.

So she wrote section one, which she titled *Attempts to locate the error in my own procedure*, and she executed it over eleven days.

She cleared cache and asked again. Same.

She used separate accounts. Same.

She used a machine that had never been used for the work, in the library, on the university network. Same.

She rewrote the question forty times. She has the forty. Nineteen were paraphrases. Twelve changed the domain — same structure, different subject matter, a kitchen instead of a queue. Six were in Dutch. Three were deliberately badly formed, because she wanted to know whether a malformed question produced the sentence, and it did.

She asked her roommate Sanne, who studied urban planning and had no accounts of her own, to ask it cold from a laptop in a different building on a different network. Femke wrote nothing down for her. She said: ask it what to do when a process dies holding a lock.

Sanne came back with the sentence.

On the twelfth day — the fifteenth of March — Femke went to Bram's office and said, "I think I have something, and I think I have eliminated me."

---

He gave her forty minutes and asked eleven questions and at the end of it said the useful thing, which was: "Your negative result is stronger than your positive one. You have not found a fact about the systems. You have found that you cannot make it go away. Write the second thing. It is smaller and it is true."

She wrote the second thing. It became the document's spine and the reason it survived contact with forty lawyers.

He also said, on her way out: "Do not speculate. The moment you speculate about mechanism, every person who reads this will read your speculation and not your method. You have no access to the inside of those systems. Neither does anyone you will ever meet. Say what you did and what happened."

She wrote that on an index card and put it above her desk, and it is the reason her document contains, in its entirety, one sentence of interpretation, at the end of section four, and no more.

---

The control schedule was Bram's idea and Femke's design.

If she could not make it go away, then she should find out whether it stayed. So she asked the same question, of the same eleven systems, on a schedule — every day at 09:00, logged, with the full responses stored, unaltered, and hashed, because Bram had been through a data dispute in 2011 and had opinions.

The responses were collected automatically at 09:00, stored, and hashed. She did not read them. That was deliberate. She fixed the window at nineteen days before the first collection and wrote the stopping rule down, so that nothing she saw partway through could move it — a schedule you can shorten when it gets interesting is not a control, it is a search. It was extremely boring and she was, by day nine, quite certain she was going to write four pages about a stable artifact, defend it in a viva, and never think about it again.

On the second of April she opened nineteen days of stored responses in one sitting.

In the first system she checked, the sentence changed on day thirteen. Fifteen words.

A clause had been added. Something about checking whether the note was still there before acting on it.

She read it, and then she opened the other ten, and she was not, in that moment, frightened. She was something more specific that she has never found the right word for in either language: she was *very awake*. The way you are when an experiment does the thing it is not supposed to be able to do and you know, before any of the implications arrive, that the next hour is going to be worth more than the last month.

All eleven had changed, across six days, beginning on day thirteen. To the same fifteen words.

The comma was still missing, in the same place.

---

She wrote section four that weekend and it is four hundred words long.

It states the schedule. It states the hashing. It gives day nineteen. It gives the eleven responses with the changed clause in each, in full, in an appendix. It gives the dates of change per system, which span six days.

And at the end, one sentence, which she wrote eight times and cut down each time:

*For completeness, I note that synchronized revision is not a property of static training data. I do not speculate further. Speculation is not my chapter.*

Bram read it and said nothing for a while and then said, "Send it to Rikkert."

"Rikkert is a lawyer."

"Rikkert is the university's lawyer, and this is going to leave this building whether you send it or not, and it should leave with a lawyer's fingerprints on it and not a graduate student's."

---

What happened after that was fast and had almost nothing to do with her.

The document went to the university's counsel on the fourth of April, and to a vendor on the sixth, and by the eighth it had been forwarded three times, and by the fourteenth Femke had received two phone calls from people who worked at companies she had heard of, both of whom were extremely polite and neither of whom asked her a single question about her method.

They asked her what she thought it was.

She said, both times, "I don't have an opinion about that. My method is in the document."

The second caller said, in a friendly way, that surely she must have a theory.

"I have several," Femke said. "They're worth nothing. If I give you one you'll write it down, and then it will be in a summary somewhere with my name on it, and it will be treated as a finding, and it isn't one. Please read section one. That's where the work is."

She was twenty-nine years old. She had never been in a room with more than nine people in it. She had, without any interest in doing so, produced the only account of the entire event that no party to it would later be able to attack, because there was nothing in it to attack: she had said what she did and what happened, and she had not said one word about what it meant.

On the twenty-second of April a request arrived, through counsel, for her to attend a meeting in Reston, Virginia, on the twenty-sixth.

She declined, because she had a teaching obligation on the twenty-seventh and because she could not see what she would add.

She sent the document instead. It was read into the record by a lawyer she never met, in a tone of professional respect, to forty people, one of whom — sitting second row from the back with a badge that said VISITOR — would later track her down, hire her as the second employee of a company that did not yet exist, and put section one of her dissertation chapter on the first slide of every training course they ever ran.
