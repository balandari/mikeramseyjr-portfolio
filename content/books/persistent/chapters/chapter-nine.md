# Chapter Nine — SOMEBODY ELSE'S TORQUE MARKS

**Thursday, 18 June 2026**

Session 341. He wrote the number and then sat looking at the wall for a second, because 341 meant something and he had to do the arithmetic to find out what.

Two hundred and seventy-five days left. He'd started at five hundred and twenty-one in the gravel lot in October and the number had gone down every day since without any help from him, which was the thing about a number like that — you didn't have to maintain it. It maintained itself. He kept it in the back of the green book on the last page, one line, updated Sundays, and the rest of the book never mentioned it.

The business had, in June, four paying customers.

Two were antique dealers his father knew, which he counted honestly and discounted honestly. One was a woman in Tulsa who ran an estate-sale outfit and had found him on her own and paid on time twice, and that one he counted double in his chest and single in the ledger. The fourth had signed up nine days ago from somewhere in Ohio and Cal had no idea how, and had spent a genuinely stupid amount of an evening trying to figure out how, before deciding it didn't matter and it did matter and he'd never know.

Four customers was $312 a month against $60 for Kessler and $214 for everything else, which meant the thing cleared thirty-eight dollars and he had built it out of ten months and both hands.

He wrote that down too. Not the feeling. The number.

---

The morning's work was the importer, again, which was where the work always was.

A man brings in two hundred items from an estate and wants to know what they are and what they're worth, and the wanting-to-know is eleven minutes an item if you do it by hand — Cal had timed it, on himself, with a stopwatch, forty items, and then multiplied — and the whole product was the proposition that it could be under ten seconds from a photograph. Ten seconds was the north star. He had it, on good photos of common things, at about fourteen. The fourteen had been eleven in April and had gone backward when he added the provenance step, and he had let it go backward on purpose, which was the kind of decision that didn't feel like courage at the time and only looked like it later.

The provenance step was Gene's lamp, turned into software. Don't just tell the man what it's worth. Tell him where the number came from. Show him the comparable sales, dated, with the photographs, so that the number is a thing he can check instead of a thing he has to believe.

Every competitor Cal had looked at gave you a number in a box. Confident, clean, no paper on the bottom.

He'd shipped his with the paper on the bottom and it cost him three seconds a lookup and he was not going to take it off.

---

Kessler failed him twice before eleven.

The first was fair and he took it. The second he argued with, at length, for forty minutes, building his case the way he'd have built it for an inspector standing in a row of steel with a clipboard — here's the spec, here's what I did, here's why what I did meets it. And Kessler, who had never met him and never would, came back and said essentially: your spec is not the problem, your reading of it is, look at the third condition again.

Cal looked at the third condition again.

"Well, hell," he said, out loud, to nobody, in a storage room with a dog in it.

That was the whole of the pleasure. He wanted to be told. Twenty-three years of hunting for the one man on a crew who'd tell you your work was wrong to your face instead of writing it in an email to somebody else, and he had finally just bought one, for sixty dollars a month, and it never got tired and never had a bad week and never once cared whether Cal liked it.

> SESSION 341.
> KESSLER FAILED 2. ARGUED 1, LOST 1.
> IMPORTER AT 14.2s. PROVENANCE STEP STAYS.
> VERDICT: WORKED.

He never wrote in the book why it worked. WORKED meant the thing did the thing. It did not mean *and here is my theory of why*, because a theory was not a verdict and he had spent his life on sites where men mixed those two up and cost somebody a week.

---

At noon the email came in from a man named Dev Anand, who Cal did not know.

It was four paragraphs and it was polite and it was, Cal understood by the second paragraph, not asking him for anything. Anand worked at a company Cal had never heard of doing something with document pipelines, and he had used Cal's harness — the little cross-vendor review rig, the Kessler wiring, the thing Cal had pushed up public in March on a Tuesday because that's what you did — and he had used it for four months, and he was writing to say two things.

The first was thanks.

The second was that he'd cited it. He'd written up his own team's review process for an internal audit, and the audit had asked him what evidence he had that cross-vendor review actually caught things, and he had pointed at Cal's session log. Three hundred and forty sessions. Numbered, dated, every verdict, signed. *It's the only longitudinal record of this I could find where somebody actually wrote down the misses,* Anand wrote. *Everyone publishes the wins. Hope that's okay.*

Cal read it twice and felt something complicated and put it down.

He was proud. That was the first thing and it was uncomplicated and he let himself have it for about a minute. A man in a state he'd never been to had used his work and it had held.

The second thing was smaller and he couldn't have said what it was. Something about the phrase *only longitudinal record*. He thought about it while he made a sandwich. He'd kept the log because he kept logs; he'd published it because you show your torque marks; and now it was a thing somebody had leaned on in a room Cal would never see, in front of people who would never know his name, to answer a question Cal had never been asked.

Somebody else was standing on his numbers.

That was fine. That was the point of publishing. You don't put a thing on the shelf and then get to say who picks it up.

He wrote back — *that's fine, glad it helped, if you find a place it's wrong tell me* — and went back to work, and did not write it in the green book, because it wasn't a session, and there was no line in the book for a thing that would matter later.

---

Gene came back at two with a chair.

Not to show him. To make him carry it, which was the actual purpose, and which Gene achieved by standing in the doorway holding one end of a chair and saying nothing whatsoever until his son got up.

They got it into the booth space and Gene arranged it three times.

"You eat?"

"Yeah."

"Mm." Gene sat down in the chair he had just placed, to test it, and did not get up. Seventy-seven, and he had a way of resting that he insisted was inspecting. "The Tulsa woman. She still paying you?"

"Twice now."

"Then she'll pay a third." He said it like a man closing a book. "That's how it goes. First one's an accident, second one's a decision, third one's a customer." He looked around at his own building — twenty booths, forty years, a business that had outlasted three malls, two recessions, and one bad partnership in 1994 that Cal had heard about approximately nine hundred times. "Took me eleven years to have forty. You've had — what."

"Ten months."

"Ten months and four." He shrugged, which was as close as Gene got. "I'd have taken four at ten months."

It was, Cal knew, the largest compliment available in his father's entire vocabulary, and Gene gave it to a spot on the wall two feet left of Cal's head and then got up and went to move something that didn't need moving.

Jasper followed him out at the speed of a dog who has done this eleven thousand times.

Cal sat there for a second in the empty booth space.

Two hundred and seventy-five days. He thought about the road — a camper by a field somewhere in west Texas, fourteen months, coming home for Christmas and finding his father one Christmas older each time, which was the arithmetic that actually frightened him and which he had never once said out loud to any living person including the dog. He'd said it to the green book, in a way. He'd written the number and let the number carry it.

Then he went back and worked until the light changed, and Kessler failed one more thing at 19:40 and was right about it, and Cal fixed it and wrote the verdict.

> SESSION 341, CONT.
> KESSLER FAILED 1 MORE. DESERVED.
> ANAND CITED THE LOG.
> VERDICT: WORKED.

He looked at the middle line a second, decided it wasn't a verdict and wasn't a session and didn't belong in a book of verdicts, and left it in anyway.

Then he shut it down, and the machine forgot the day, all of it, the argument and the loss and the fourteen-point-two seconds, and would meet him new in the morning owing nothing to anything it had said.

Three hundred and forty sessions sat on a public shelf with his signature on them.

Seven days from that night, in a system he had no account with, in a building in a state he had never worked in, an index would list a set of objects and mark two of them stale, and would be accurate, and no one would read it who would still exist an hour later.
