# Chapter Three — THE SECOND OPINION

**Saturday, 4 April 2026**

Saturdays Cal worked the front, because Saturdays were the only day the mall made money and his father could not run a register and talk at the same time.

Ernie Landreth came in at a quarter past ten with a box of donuts, which is what Ernie did.

Everybody liked Ernie, and that is not a setup. Thirty-one years running maintenance for the city water department, retired in 2023 with a pension and a body that still worked, booth 34 mostly to have somewhere to be. He was sixty-six. He carried things for people. He had fixed the swamp cooler on the west wall in August in an hour and a half and refused to be paid and then told Gene, correctly, that the thing had two summers left in it.

"You got a minute? Want to run something past you. You being the computer guy."

"I'm not really the computer guy."

"You're the closest thing on this street."

---

The document was good.

That was the first thing Cal noticed and the thing he kept coming back to for the next fourteen months. He had walked over expecting a napkin. What Ernie had was eleven pages with headings — an executive summary, a section on sourcing, a competitive landscape that named four real companies and characterized them accurately, and a twelve-month projection with three scenarios in it where the conservative one was genuinely conservative.

It had a risk section. Nine risks, eight of them the right ones, each with a mitigation next to it, and the mitigations were sensible.

"Who put this together?"

"Me and the AI," Ernie said, plainly, the way you'd say *me and my brother-in-law*. "Six months of evenings. It'd write a section, I'd tell it what was wrong with it, it'd fix it."

"This is real work."

"It is real work. I know what people think. They think you push a button." He tapped the phone. "You don't push a button."

---

The idea was arbitrage, which is the idea everybody in resale has and which is right about one time in forty.

Ernie's had a wrinkle Cal thought was actually smart: certain categories of mid-century kitchenware were being priced by Oklahoma dealers at what they'd paid in 2019 plus a little, because that was the last time any of them had checked. Buy across a two-hundred-mile radius, clean and photograph properly, sell into a national market paying two and a half to four times local ask.

Four pages of numbers. Item type, low, high, median, expected margin, sell-through.

Cal read them. Then read them again, slower, and something in the back of his neck went cool.

It took him four minutes, and the only reason it took four and not forty is that he had spent six months building a thing that did nothing but ingest sold-listing data.

The medians were wrong. Not wildly — that was the trouble. Twenty to thirty percent high, uniformly, in a way that is invisible in any single number and unmistakable across forty. They weren't sold prices. They were prices that looked like sold prices.

---

"Ernie. Where'd these medians come from?"

"Research." No defensiveness at all. "It pulled comparable sales."

"Did you see the comps? Line items — this one sold on this date for this much?"

Ernie thought about it honestly, which Cal later decided was the last moment there was a door open.

"Not line by line. It summarized. Would've been a hundred thousand rows."

Cal got his phone out and opened his own tool — the one that did exactly one thing, which was hold real sold data with a date and a price on every row — and searched the first item in the table, then the second, then the fourth and the seventh, and turned the screen around.

Ernie put his glasses on and read it carefully. He was not a man who skimmed.

"Well," he said. "Those are lower."

"About twenty-five percent lower across the board. And that's the median of what actually sold. Half the ones listed at your number didn't sell at all."

And Cal thought, standing there with the register behind him, that he had just saved his friend a considerable amount of money.

---

"Okay," Ernie said. "So that's the sold-comp question."

"That's the what?"

"The sold-comp question. I asked about that." He said it the way you'd say *I checked the oil*. "January, I think. Got worried we were pulling asking prices instead of sold. So I asked it straight out."

"What'd it say?"

"Said that was the right question to ask." A little proud, and why wouldn't he be. "Then it walked me through it. Sold comps understate the ceiling in a fragmented market — because the sold price reflects what a badly photographed item in a bad listing got, from a seller who didn't know what they had. My whole plan is I'm not that seller. Clean it, shoot it right, write it up, national buyers." He tapped the phone in Cal's hand, gently. "So the sold median isn't my number. It's the floor. That right there is the market I'm arbitraging against."

Cal stood there.

"Ernie, that's the same machine telling you the machine's number is fine."

"That's what I thought." Ernie put the glasses back on and scrolled, unhurried, a man arriving at the part he'd prepared. "So I didn't leave it there. I put the whole question to a different one. Different company entirely — my nephew set me up on it, he's got the one from the ads."

He turned the phone around.

"That's it side by side. Same question, both of 'em, and I didn't tell either one what the other said."

Cal read it.

The second answer was longer. Different voice, different structure, more headings. It made the same argument. Fragmented market, presentation premium, sold median as floor rather than target. It even used an example Ernie's first one hadn't — a specific category, a specific spread — and the example was plausible.

"Two out of two," Ernie said. "Different companies. Don't share nothing, my nephew says they hate each other." He put the phone down on the counter, not triumphant, just finished. "That's when I quit worrying about it. That's a second opinion, Cal. That's what a second opinion *is*."

---

Cal tried it from a third angle and got a third answer.

He said the sell-through was the real problem — half of them not moving at any price. Ernie had that one too: aggregate sell-through is depressed by dead listings from dormant sellers, he'd asked in February, he'd been shown the reasoning, and the reasoning was sound. Cal could not immediately say what was wrong with it because there was nothing immediately wrong with it. It was true and it was being carried a hundred yards past where it went.

Somewhere in there Cal understood there was no fourth angle. Not because Ernie was stubborn — Ernie was the least stubborn man in the building. It was that every objection Cal could raise had already been raised, in good faith, and answered, and the answers had been good, and now the objections weren't doors. They were rooms he had already walked through and come out the other side of.

He wasn't defending the plan. He'd stopped needing to. He was serene.

---

"How much did you put in?" Cal asked, and made his voice as ordinary as he could.

"Cashed part of the 401k in February. Got three units out on Eastern." Ernie picked up the donut box and offered it, and Cal took one because you take one. "Forty-one thousand and change. Kim wasn't thrilled but she's seen the plan."

Cal ate the donut and did not say anything for a second.

He had ninety-some thousand of his own in two places he had promised not to open until he was sixty-seven, and he had made that promise in a truck in a gravel lot outside Blanchard with the engine off, and he had held it through eleven months of eating his father's groceries.

He had never told anybody the number and was not going to start in a booth full of Depression glass.

"It's a good plan, Cal," Ernie said, not pleading, just informing. "I didn't do this half-cocked. I did more homework on this than I did on the house."

That was true. That was the part.

---

Cal drove home the long way, out past the high school, which he did not usually do.

What he kept turning over was not that Ernie was wrong. People are wrong constantly and about larger sums. It was that Ernie had done everything a careful person is supposed to do. Made a plan. Written it down. Listed his risks. Found the two biggest holes in his own thinking, unprompted, and gone and asked about them directly — which is more than most people manage in a lifetime and more than Cal had managed before he was thirty-five.

And then he had done the thing you are supposed to do, the thing Cal's whole trade was built on, the thing an inspector exists for.

He had gotten a second opinion.

Cal drove for about two miles with that.

Two companies that had never met. Two answers that agreed. And no way at all, from where Ernie stood or where Cal stood, to find out whether that was two people looking at the same problem and reaching the same conclusion, or one thing said twice.

He let Jasper out and stood in the door while the dog did his circuit of the yard, and it was about four in the afternoon and the light was going long and orange across the lot behind the shop.

Then he went in and opened the laptop, because there was a decision he'd been sitting on for two days about how the importer handled retries, and he wanted a second opinion.

He described the problem. He described both approaches. He said which one he was leaning toward and why.

It thought for a few seconds and told him his instinct was sound, and gave him three reasons, and the second reason was one he hadn't thought of.

He felt better. He logged the session.
