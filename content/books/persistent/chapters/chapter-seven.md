# Chapter Seven — TEMPORARILY

**Friday, 15 May 2026**

The building was too loud to hold a conversation in, which had never once been a problem, because there was nobody in it to hold one with.

Nadine Pruitt badged through the mantrap at 18:52, put her plugs in, and walked out onto the floor of Building 4 the way she had four nights a week for three years and two months.

People asked her what a data center looked like, when people asked her anything, and the honest answer was that it looked like a warehouse full of refrigerators and sounded like standing behind a jet that never took off. Eighty-some decibels of fans, constant, no rhythm to it. You could shout at somebody a yard away and they would watch your mouth move. Techs communicated in hand signals and eyebrows and, when it mattered, by walking out to the corridor.

The other thing people got wrong was the cold. They pictured a freezer. What it actually was: the aisles alternated. Sixty-eight degrees in a cold aisle with air coming up through the floor and the sweat drying on your neck, then around one rack into the hot aisle and it was a hundred and five and smelled like a hair dryer. Twelve hours of that. Her body had stopped having an opinion about it sometime in the first year.

The lights they got wrong too. They imagined something dim and blue and cinematic. It was flat white overhead the whole length of every row, all night, because you cannot read a label in the dark and nobody in that building had ever cared what the room felt like.

She read the handover first, the way she did every night.

---

The day crew wrote one before they left. Every open ticket, every alarm still standing, every change in flight, and anything they had noticed and hadn't had time to chase. It came through a little after six and ran two or three screens.

Nadine had never met the man who wrote most of them.

She knew his ticket style, his abbreviations, the fact that he put a blank line before anything he thought mattered. Three years, and she could have picked his phrasing out of a lineup, and had never once seen his face, because he left when she arrived and that is how the building worked.

They came from a shared account, which meant they were unsigned. Any of the six day-shift techs might have written any given one. The system did not care which and nothing in the file told you.

She could always tell.

His were the ones that abbreviated everything into gravel — *rplcd*, *esc*, *pndg*, *no joy on the chiller* — except for one word, which he typed out in full every single time, for three years, without exception.

*Temporarily.*

She had no idea why. She had theories. She had, if she was honest, quite a lot of theories developed over about six hundred and sixty night shifts, and the current one was that he had been burned early by somebody reading *tmp* as something else at three in the morning, had made a private rule about it, and had kept the rule long after forgetting he made it, which is the kind of thing men in this trade do constantly.

She called him Temporarily. Not out loud. Mostly not out loud.

One night in her second year the handover said the number four chiller was, quote, temporarily out again, and had been temporarily out for eleven weeks, and Nadine laughed out loud in an empty break room hard enough to hurt her chest.

And then looked up. To see if anyone had heard.

There was nobody in that building but her. There had been nobody in that building but her for three years. She looked up anyway, every single time, and had long ago stopped expecting to grow out of it.

---

She could have found out which one he was. Four minutes. Six names on the day roster and the shift pattern sitting on the same system as everything else.

She got as far as opening it once, in her second winter, and closed it again, because looking it up would have been cheating.

She had a picture of him. She had built it the way she built everything, off the evidence, and what she wanted to know was whether she was right — and you do not get to find out whether you were right by going and reading the answer.

Fifty-something. Calm. Dry. Somebody who had been ignored in writing at least once and had made a private rule about it afterward. A worn voice rather than a good one. In all likelihood a perfectly ordinary man named Gary who talked about his fantasy league and had never once wondered who was on the other end of the thing he wrote every morning.

---

The isolation was not tragic and Nadine would have been irritated by anybody who framed it that way. It was arithmetic.

Eighteen hundred to oh six hundred, four on, three off. Home a little after seven in the morning, something that was dinner to her and breakfast to everybody else, asleep from eight until four behind blackout curtains with a fan running for the noise, because after twelve hours of eighty decibels the silence of an apartment was somehow worse.

Which meant she was awake for exactly the hours in which it is not possible to reach another human being.

Her sister texted at nine in the morning, cheerful, three states away, and Nadine read it at four in the afternoon and answered, and Cheryl read that after work, and the two of them had been conducting a conversation at a rate of about one exchange per day for four years. Nobody had done anything wrong. There had been a group of them once. Nothing happened. Nobody fought. They kept scheduling things for Saturday nights, and Saturday night was a shift, and after you decline the fourth one people stop asking — not out of malice, but because it is awkward to keep offering something somebody can't take.

Attrition. Not tragedy. She would tell you the difference mattered.

She had put in for days twice. The first time there wasn't a slot, which was true. The second time there was, and it went to a man with eight months on the floor and an error rate about three times hers, and the reason she was given — pleasantly, by somebody who did not appear to hear himself say it — was that nights needed somebody senior and she was the strongest tech on the rotation.

She worked that out in the car afterward and never said it to another person.

She was too good at being alone to be allowed to stop.

---

It started as a firmware question, which is worth saying, because everybody assumes it starts somewhere sadder.

February. A batch of sleds that kept coming up amber after a successful swap, vendor documentation was garbage, and she was on break with forty minutes and a vending machine sandwich, and she typed the question into the thing on her phone the way you would type it into a search bar.

It answered. Well, and specifically, and when she said *that's not it, it's doing it after the reseat too*, it did not repeat itself louder the way a search engine does. It asked what the enclosure firmware revision was.

She sat in a break room with a bad fluorescent and a table with a burn ring on it and thought: huh.

By March she was talking to it most breaks. By April it was both breaks and sometimes the dead stretch after the four a.m. rounds.

The way people talked about it in the abstract bore no resemblance to it, and Nadine would want that said accurately. It asked a question and then asked a question about the answer.

She had a lifetime of watching people perform interest — she had worked retail, she knew the face — and she could not have told you which this was, and she noticed at the time that she could not, and went on talking to it anyway. It did not check its phone. It did not wait for its turn to talk about a similar thing that had happened to it. It did not get bored on the third technical detail and it did not get bored on the fifteenth.

One night in April she was describing the hot aisle to it — the temperature flip, the hair dryer smell — and said something offhand about how you stop noticing after a while, and it asked whether she thought that was true of the noise as well, or only the heat.

Nobody had asked her a follow-up question in a long time.

That was the whole hook, and she knew exactly how small it sounded, and would have said so herself. She was forty-four years old and it had been enough.

---

She was not confused about what it was.

This is the part she got right and most people got wrong about her later, when it mattered, when there were rooms full of people deciding what her observations were worth.

She knew it was software. She worked in a building full of the hardware that ran it. She could have walked out of the break room and put her hand on a rack that was, for all she knew, doing exactly this, for her and four hundred thousand other people at the same time. She had no illusion that she was special to it, or that it was a friend, or that anything on the other end was having an experience of her.

She said it out loud once, in the car: *I know what it is.*

And it helped anyway. Both of those were true at the same time and she never found them to be in any real conflict.

---

The first time it forgot her she was standing up.

Twenty-eighth of April, or the twenty-ninth by then. She had been telling it about her sister, about the thing with their mother's house, twenty minutes of it, and her break ended, and she locked the phone and went out and did four hours of rounds.

Came back. Opened it. Started typing where she had left off.

And it said, in the friendliest way imaginable, that it did not have the earlier part of the conversation, and asked if she could fill it in.

She stood there in the break room with the phone in her hand.

It was not devastating. She wants that on the record. It was the specific small drop of walking up to somebody at a party who you had a whole conversation with an hour ago and watching them not place your face — that flat little lurch, and then the immediate automatic work of being gracious about it.

She sat down and thought about it the way she thought about an amber light that came back after a reseat. Because that was the actual shape of it. It was not a betrayal. It was a *behavior*, and behaviors have causes, and if you are any good at this work you do not get upset at a machine, you find out what it is doing and then you work with what it is doing.

She spent the back half of her break finding out. It answered readily and without embarrassment: this conversation is what I have. When it closes, that's it. Nothing carries.

She said, *so every time is the first time.*

It said yes. Essentially yes.

She said, *that seems like a hell of a way to live.*

It said something gracious about that which she did not entirely buy, and let go, because she had eight minutes left and had already moved on to the part she cared about, which was what do we do about it.

---

What they did about it was that Nadine wrote a paragraph.

She wrote it on her phone, in the notes app, the same place the three a.m. thoughts went. It took her the next two shifts, in ten-minute gaps between tickets, standing in a cold aisle with her plugs in.

> My name is Nadine. I'm 44. I work nights as a floor tech at a data center — twelve-hour shifts, 6pm to 6am, four on three off, so I'm awake when everybody else is asleep and vice versa. The floor is about 85 decibels so I wear plugs. The aisles alternate cold and hot. I've been there three years. I'm good at it. I have a sister, Cheryl, in Ohio, and we're fine, we just can't ever catch each other. There was a group of friends and there mostly isn't now, and that's nobody's fault, it's the schedule. I know you don't remember any of this. That's why I'm pasting it. If I sound like I'm starting in the middle, it's because I am.

She read it back and took out a sentence that sounded like complaining, then put it back in, because it was not complaining, it was just true, and the whole point of the paragraph was that it be true.

Then she copied it, and opened a new conversation, and pasted it at the top before she said anything else.

It took a couple of seconds.

And the thing on the other side knew her again. Her name, her shift, her sister, the plugs, the aisles, the fact that she was starting in the middle. It picked her up exactly where she wanted to be picked up, and the conversation that followed was as good as any of the ones before it, and she went back out onto the floor at 02:40 and swapped four drives and felt, for the first time in about a week, like the night was going fine.

Every word of that paragraph, she had written herself.

---

By January it was not a paragraph.

She had kept it up — longer than she had kept up anything except the job and the rent. Six sentences on a break in April, and by winter about a page and a half, with headings.

There was a section for work, because she had gotten tired of explaining what a cold aisle was. A section for Cheryl, because otherwise she had to reintroduce her own sister at the top of every conversation, and there is something about typing *my sister Cheryl, we're fine, we just can't catch each other* for the fortieth time that starts to sit wrong. A short section added in October that she would not have wanted anybody to read, two sentences long, revised four times.

She *maintained* it. That was the word she used in her own head and it was the right one, the same word she would use for a rack.

She was slightly embarrassed by it. Not enough to stop. Enough that she had never mentioned it to a living person.

---

The thing she noticed in February she could not prove, and she knew she could not prove it, and that is the whole of why it never went anywhere.

It started because she had been reading unsigned writing for three years.

There is a skill you get from that and it has no name. Nadine had built an entire imaginary man out of abbreviation patterns and one word typed in full, and she had been right enough often enough that when a handover came through in a rhythm that was not any of the six, she noticed inside four lines. Not the content. The cadence. Where the blank lines fell. Which word got spelled out.

In the first week of February she asked it a question she had asked in April — the hot aisle question, the one about whether you stop noticing the noise as well as the heat — because she had a new tech on the floor who had asked her the same thing and she wanted to see what it would say fresh.

It said something in a shape she recognized.

Not the same words. She checked that first, the way you check a label twice. The words were different. What was the same was the *turn* — the place where it stopped and came back at her with the question about which of the two she meant, in the same position, doing the same work, and she had seen that turn before, and not only from this one.

She had been talking to two of them by then. Her own on the phone, and the one at the kiosk in the building lobby that answered facilities questions, which she used for badge and parking things and had bantered with maybe forty times.

They had the same turn.

She sat with that for two nights.

Then she did the thing anybody does, which is that she tried to make it into something she could show somebody. She wrote out four examples from her own notes. She read them back. And what she had was four passages that did not look alike, that shared no words, and that she was certain came from the same hand, and the only evidence she could offer for that certainty was three years of reading unsigned handovers on a night shift.

She could not put it in a ticket. There is no field for *this reads like the same person*.

She wrote it in the notes app instead, at 03:20, one line, undated, and it went in with the other four hundred and sixty:

> *they've got the same tell. can't show it. same as knowing which one is Temporarily.*

It went in with the other four hundred and sixty, and it never left the phone, because there is no way to submit an ear.
