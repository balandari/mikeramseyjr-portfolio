# Chapter Twenty-One — NO CLIENTS

**March – April 2027**

The first thing she learned was that there is no such job.

She had known that abstractly on the twenty-fourth of February and she learned it concretely over the following six weeks, which she spent doing the only thing available to a person with a discipline and no customers, which was writing the discipline down.

The methodology document was eighty-one pages by the end of March and nobody had asked for it.

She wrote it the way she wrote everything: numbered sections, confidence stated per claim, a section on what the method could not do that was longer than the section on what it could. She had one working title for eleven days — *Independent Verification Protocol* — and then threw it out, because *protocol* implied somebody was following it, and nobody was, and she was not going to put a definite label on an indefinite thing.

She retitled it *Notes Toward Checking Things You Have a Reason to Believe*.

Wes read the first forty pages on a Saturday and said, "This is good, and I don't know who this is for."

"Neither do I."

"Is that a problem?"

"It's the entire problem," she said, and went back to work.

---

The money was the part she did not talk about.

She had Anvil equity, vested, and she had been selling it in tranches since March — a tranche a quarter, on a plan, eleven months of runway if nothing changed. In March she moved to a tranche a month, because things had changed, and she did the arithmetic at the kitchen table on the back of a receipt, and she wrote the number down, and she drew a box around it, and then she sat looking at the box for a while.

The runway was denominated in the company she was investigating.

Not investigating, exactly. Anvil wasn't the source; Anvil was a customer of the thing, like everyone else. But the finding, if it ever became a public finding, was going to be bad for every company in the chain, and Anvil was in the chain, and the stock that was paying her rent while she wrote eighty-one pages about independence would be worth less the day she succeeded.

She had spent three days in February establishing that she could not investigate this from inside a company that had a stake in the answer.

She was now funding the investigation out of a position in a company that had a stake in the answer.

She looked at that for a long time and could not make it not true. What she did instead was the only thing she'd ever found that worked: she wrote it down, in the document, in section one, under a heading she typed and then sat back from.

**1.4 — The verifier's own position.**

*I hold equity in a company affected by the outcome of this work. I am selling it to fund this work. This is a stake. It cannot be eliminated. It can be disclosed, and it is disclosed here, and any reader of any finding I produce is entitled to weigh it.*

*Disclosure is not neutralization. I do not claim to be unaffected. I claim only that you now know what I am, and can grade my work accordingly.*

She never told Wes the number. That was the one thing she kept, and she knew she was keeping it, and she wrote that down too, later, in a different document, years afterward, when she was teaching this.

---

In the first week of April she went looking for evidence that cross-checking worked at all.

This was, she realized, the hole in her whole enterprise. She was proposing that things be checked by parties with no stake in the answer. She believed it the way you believe something you have paid for personally. She had zero evidence that it produced better outcomes than checking things yourself, because nobody publishes the counterfactual, and every vendor white paper on review practices was a document written by people whose product was review.

Everyone published the wins. She needed somebody who had written down the misses.

She spent four days looking. She found conference talks, marketing, two academic papers with sample sizes in the low dozens, and one meta-analysis that concluded more study was needed.

Then, on the eighth of April, from a citation in an audit write-up that somebody had posted publicly for unrelated reasons, she found a repository.

---

It was not impressive to look at.

A small tool for wiring a review model from one vendor against work produced by another. Plain documentation, written by someone with no interest whatsoever in being read charitably. About four hundred people had it starred.

Packaged with it was a log.

Six hundred and eleven sessions. Numbered, dated, each with a one-line verdict in a fixed vocabulary of two words, WORKED and FAILED, and — she scrolled, and kept scrolling, and then went back to the top and started again with a different kind of attention — the failures were in it.

Not summarized. Listed. *KESSLER FAILED 3, DESERVED 2, ARGUED 1 AND LOST.* *ARGUED 1, LOST 1.* *FAILED 1 MORE. DESERVED.* Every entry dated. Every entry signed, at the bottom of the file, with a key, by one person.

Maya sat back in her chair in an apartment with two unpacked boxes now unpacked, and said, out loud, to nobody, "Oh, you *idiot*."

She meant it with enormous affection. She meant: you have published, for free, with your name on it, the only honest longitudinal dataset in this entire field, and you have done it by accident, because you are a person who writes things down, and you do not appear to have the first idea what you have handed the world.

She read all six hundred and eleven entries in one sitting. It took her five hours. She made a spreadsheet, because she counted things.

The numbers were not spectacular and that was the point. Cross-vendor review caught things same-vendor review didn't at a rate she could actually compute, with a denominator, over twelve months, on real work by one operator who had no incentive to make it look good. There were streaks where it caught nothing. There were four entries where the operator had beaten it and said so.

It was the best evidence she had ever seen for the thing she had quit her job over, and it had been sitting in public for a year, and as far as she could tell she was the first person on earth to read it as evidence.

---

She did not contact him.

She thought about it, on the ninth, and wrote out the email twice and sent neither, for a reason she could articulate precisely, which was: *I have a use for this man's data and no standing to ask him for anything, and if I write to him now I am a stranger with a thesis asking him to confirm it.*

That was the shape of a bad question. She knew what bad questions did. She had spent eleven years learning that you do not go to a source with the answer already in your hand, because sources are kind, and kind people help you find what you're looking for.

So she cited it instead. Section 3.2, with a link, with the caveats stated — single operator, single domain, self-reported, no control — and a line she wrote carefully:

*This is the only dataset I have located in which the misses are recorded. Its author appears to have kept it for his own reasons and published it without commentary. I have not contacted him. I did not want to shape it.*

Seven weeks later she would write to him, and it would not be to ask for anything. The asking came in July, and by then she would go in with the answer already in her hand.

She would have written 3.2 by then anyway. She never took it out.

---

On the second of April a woman in Delft whose name Maya did not yet know finished the nineteenth day of a control schedule and observed that the sentence had changed.

On the twenty-second, counsel Maya had never met sent an invitation to an address she had listed on an eighty-one page document that nobody had commissioned.

The invitation was worded so that nobody had to write down the reason for it.

Her badge, when she got there, would say VISITOR, and it would be the only accurate badge in the room: the only person present with no employer whose stock moved on the answer, and she had bought that condition with a tranche a month, and it had cost her exactly the amount she had written on a receipt and boxed and never said out loud.
